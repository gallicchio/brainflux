Start here

A quantum particle in one dimension, already running. The coloured hump is where the particle is likely to be found, the grey curve is the potential it lives in, and the colours are the phase of its wave function: stripes mean it is moving. Below are the same wave function in momentum space and a space-time picture that scrolls as time goes on.

Pick a potential on the right, then a starting wave function from its rows of named states, and drag the sliders (x₀, σ, k₀ for a packet). Click a thin grey line on the plot to load that stationary state. Play pauses and resumes, Restart goes back to the beginning, Backwards runs time in reverse (it pauses at time zero, the starting state again; Play then goes on into negative time, the state's past), and Speed slows a fast packet down.

Look for this: a stationary state only changes colour; anything else moves, spreads or sloshes. The triangle and bar at the foot of the plot are the mean position and its spread, and the readouts above the plot give the same numbers. Each experiment below sets up one idea: press Set it up.

The time-dependent Schrödinger equation $$i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2}+V(x,t)\,\psi$$ with $\hbar=m=1$ (the mass is a slider under Domain and numerics). The filled curve is $|\psi|^2$, the probability density, coloured by the phase of $\psi$ (the colour wheel below); the grey region is $V(x)$ (an infinite wall is a grey band), read on the right-hand energy axis, with the stationary states' energies as thin lines and $\langle E\rangle$ dashed. The triangle at the foot is $\langle x\rangle$, and the bar through it spans $\langle x\rangle\pm\sigma_x$. Under it is the wave function in momentum space, $\phi(p)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int\psi(x)\,e^{-ipx/\hbar}\,dx$, drawn the same way, with $\langle p\rangle\pm\sigma_p$.

Pick a potential and an initial state, then press Play. The initial states come in four kinds, a row each: a Gaussian packet, a stationary state, an equal mix of two, and a formula you type. Each row starts with its kind, then named starting points of that kind; the highlighted button is the state loaded. Move one of its sliders and the highlight moves to the kind: the state is now your own. Typed expressions may use any symbols: each new one gets a slider. Change the potential while the clock runs, and the wave function feels it suddenly.

1. A free particle spreads

A narrow packet at rest. Its width grows as $\sigma(t)=\sigma_0\sqrt{1+(\hbar t/2m\sigma_0^2)^2}$: the narrower it starts, the faster it spreads, because a narrow packet is made of a wide range of momenta. Watch $\sigma_x\sigma_p$ in the readout: it starts at its minimum, $\hbar/2$, and grows, while $\sigma_p$ itself never changes (no force).

2. The phase is the momentum

A packet moving to the right has colours cycling along it, once every $2\pi/k_0$ (3.1 here, for $k_0=2$): $k_0$ is the wavenumber, and the momentum is $\hbar k_0$. Drag the $k_0$ slider through zero: the colour stripes stop, then cycle the other way, and the packet goes left. The momentum panel shows the same thing: $|\phi(p)|^2$ is centred on $\hbar k_0$.

3. Two packets interfere

Two packets moving towards each other. Where they overlap, $|\psi_1+\psi_2|^2$ has fringes $|\psi_1|^2+|\psi_2|^2+2\,\mathrm{Re}\,\psi_1^*\psi_2$, spaced by $\pi/k_0$. They pass through each other unchanged: the equation is linear.

4. Stationary states, and what moves

The second state of the infinite well. $|\psi|^2$ does not change at all; only the phase turns, as $e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}$ (the colours cycle). Now choose 1 + 2: a superposition of two energies sloshes from side to side with period $2\pi\hbar/(E_2-E_1)$ (42 here). Only superpositions of different energies move. The bars at the right edge show the state's energy distribution $|c_n|^2$; they never change.

5. Quantum revival

A packet bouncing in a box. It spreads and seems to dissolve into a mess, yet at $T_\text{rev}=4mL^2/\pi\hbar\approx127$ it reassembles exactly, and at fractions of $T_\text{rev}$ it splits into several copies. The reason: the energies $n^2\pi^2/2L^2$ are all whole multiples of one energy, so all the phases line up again. The space-time picture (bottom) draws this as a "quantum carpet".

6. A coherent state: almost classical

A Gaussian with the ground state's width, $\sigma=\sqrt{\hbar/2m\omega}=0.71$, displaced to $x_0=3$ in a harmonic well. It swings back and forth without changing shape (the bar $\langle x\rangle\pm\sigma_x$ keeps its length), with $\langle x\rangle$ (the triangle) following the classical particle (the red dot, under Show: Classical) exactly: for a quadratic potential Ehrenfest's theorem $\frac{d}{dt}\langle p\rangle=-\langle V'(x)\rangle$ is Newton's law. Try the V-shaped potential with the same packet: there $\langle V'\rangle\ne V'(\langle x\rangle)$ and the two part ways.

7. A squeezed state breathes

Narrower than the ground state ($\sigma=0.3$ against its 0.71) and at rest in the middle, this packet stays put, $\langle x\rangle=\langle p\rangle=0$, but its width oscillates at twice the oscillator's frequency, trading position uncertainty for momentum uncertainty and back: the bars $\pm\sigma_x$ and $\pm\sigma_p$ take turns to be long. $\sigma_x\sigma_p$ is back at its minimum, $\hbar/2$, whenever $\sigma_x$ is at its largest or smallest.

8. Tunnelling

The packet's energy is below the top of the barrier ($\langle E\rangle$ dashed, under the grey), yet part of it gets through. $P(x>0)$ in the readout is the probability on the far side: once the packet has gone it is the transmission, 0.39 here (the plane-wave $T$ at this energy is 0.39 too). Widen the barrier: the transmission falls roughly as $e^{-2\kappa a}$, with $\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar$. Inside the barrier the wave function decays without oscillating: no colour stripes. Choose probability current under Show: $j=\frac{\hbar}{m}\mathrm{Im}\,\psi^*\psi'$ is drawn blue where probability flows to the right and red where it flows to the left; it pours through the barrier, and its integral over time at a point is the probability that has passed that point. (A bound stationary state is real up to a phase and carries no current at all.)

9. Reflection from a step, even going over it

Above the step, a classical particle would always go on; a wave is partly reflected, with $R=\left(\frac{k-k'}{k+k'}\right)^2$, $k'=\sqrt{k^2-2mV_0/\hbar^2}$ the wavenumber over the step. Here $k_0=2.4$ and $V_0=2$, so $k'=1.33$ and $R=0.083$; $P(x<0)$ ends a little higher, at 0.094, because the slower parts of the packet reflect more. Choose Below the step: everything comes back, after a short excursion into the forbidden region.

10. Bound states leak into the walls

A finite well has a finite number of bound states (here 3). Each one reaches into the classically forbidden region beyond the walls, decaying as $e^{-\kappa|x|}$: the higher the state, the farther. Click a level (a thin line) to load that state.

11. Passing over a well: resonance

A particle passing over the well is partly reflected, by the jump in $V$ at each edge. Over the well its wavenumber is larger, $k'=\sqrt{k^2+2mV_0/\hbar^2}$, and the waves reflected at the two edges cancel when the well is a whole number of half wavelengths wide, $2ak'=n\pi$: then it all goes through. In general $$T=\left[1+\frac{V_0^2\sin^2(2ak')}{4E(E+V_0)}\right]^{-1}.$$ This packet has $k_0=2.865$, so $2ak'=3\pi$, and passes almost entirely: $P(x>0)$ ends at 0.985. Now choose Off resonance: $k_0=1.19$, halfway to the resonance below ($2ak'=2.5\pi$), and more than two thirds comes back ($T=0.31$). The packets are wide ($\sigma=2.5$) so that their spread in $k$, $1/2\sigma$, is narrow. Slow electrons crossing noble-gas atoms almost unscattered (the Ramsauer–Townsend effect) are the same cancellation.

12. Tunnelling back and forth

A particle in the left well of a double well is the superposition $(\psi_0+\psi_1)/\sqrt2$ of the two lowest states, which differ in energy by a tiny splitting $\Delta E$. It tunnels to the right well and back with period $2\pi\hbar/\Delta E$ (here $\Delta E=0.112$, so 56). Raise the barrier: the splitting shrinks exponentially, and the tunnelling slows. This is the ammonia molecule's inversion and the qubit's two states.

13. The delta function

An attractive $V=-\alpha\,\delta(x)$ has exactly one bound state, $\psi=\sqrt\alpha\,e^{-\alpha|x|}$ with $E=-\alpha^2/2$. Here the delta is a narrow Gaussian well of width $w$ and area $\alpha$, and its level sits a little higher, at $-0.465$: the true bound state has a cusp at the delta, made of very short wavelengths, which a well of any width blunts. A packet sent at it is transmitted with probability close to $T=k^2/(k^2+\alpha^2)$: one half for $k=\alpha$ (0.51 here, for the same reason: watch $P(x>0)$ once the packet has gone). The slower the packet, the less it can tell the well from a true delta.

14. The quantum bouncer

A ball dropped onto a floor in uniform gravity, $V=gx$. The red dot is the classical ball. The quantum one keeps time for a few bounces, then dissolves, and later partly revives. The stationary states are Airy functions. Choose Typed: $xe^{-bx}$ is the textbook variational guess for the lowest one. Drag $b$ and watch $\langle E\rangle=\hbar^2b^2/2m+3g/2b$: it is least, 1.966, at $b=1.145$, just above the true ground state, 1.856 (the lowest level), as the variational principle promises.

15. Bloch oscillations

An electron in a crystal ($V_0\cos 2\pi x/a$) pulled by a constant force (the $Fx$ in $V$: the force is $-F$, to the left) does not accelerate forever: it oscillates, with period $2\pi\hbar/Fa$ (25 here, swinging about 16 to the left), because its momentum runs to the edge of the Brillouin zone and is Bragg-reflected. Set $F=0$ to see it simply spread. A little of the packet leaks to higher bands each time (Zener tunnelling); a smaller $V_0$ leaks more.

16. Driving at resonance

The ground state of a harmonic oscillator pushed by $F\cos\omega t$. At resonance ($\omega=1$) the packet swings ever wider without changing shape: it is a coherent state whose amplitude grows linearly. Off resonance it beats.

17. A sudden change

The ground state of a narrow box. While it runs, drag the box's width $L$: the walls move and the wave function, unchanged at that instant, is now a superposition of the new box's states (watch the bars at the right). Move the slider slowly instead and it stays in the ground state: the adiabatic theorem.

18. Measurement

Press Position under Make a measurement: a position is chosen at random with probability $|\psi|^2$, and the wave function collapses to a narrow packet there ($\sigma=0.05$, a two-hundredth of the box), which then spreads. Press Energy: one stationary state is chosen with probability $|c_n|^2$ and the wave function becomes it, motionless. Measure the energy twice: the same answer.

19. Running time backwards

A spread-out mess: a packet hit a barrier a while ago, and part went through, part came back. Backwards is on: press Play, and the reflected and transmitted parts come back together into the original packet, and the clock pauses at $t=0$. The Schrödinger equation is time-reversal symmetric (the absorbing walls are not, so they are off here). Press Play again to go on into negative time, the packet's past under the same Hamiltonian: it moves back to the left and is wider, so at $t=0$ it was at its narrowest (a Gaussian with no chirp spreads the same way into the past as into the future, $\sigma(t)=\sigma_0\sqrt{1+(\hbar t/2m\sigma_0^2)^2}$). In this box its past soon includes a bounce off the left wall; with absorbing walls the past would be wrong, as what they take can't come back.

How it is computed

The split-operator method: each step multiplies $\psi$ by $e^{-iV\Delta t/2\hbar}$ in position space, by $e^{-i\hbar k^2\Delta t/2m}$ in momentum space (one FFT there and one back), and by $e^{-iV\Delta t/2\hbar}$ again. Every factor has magnitude 1, so probability is conserved exactly, the kinetic energy is exact, and the error per step is of order $\Delta t^3$. The stationary states are found separately, by a finite-difference eigenvalue solver. Walls are infinitely high; ring joins the ends; absorbing edges swallow what reaches them.

Perfectly sharp steps are an idealisation the method pays for with tiny time steps, so a potential with jumps loses its Fourier components above $k=16$ (reflection and tunnelling for $k<5$ are those of the sharp step), and walls taller than 30 are rounded over 0.05. The time step adapts to the potential where the wave function is.
