Experiments

The time-dependent Schrödinger equation $$i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2}+V(x,t)\,\psi$$ with $\hbar=m=1$. The filled curve is $|\psi|^2$, the probability density, coloured by the phase of $\psi$ (the colour wheel below); the grey region is $V(x)$, read on the right-hand energy axis, with the stationary states' energies as thin lines and $\langle E\rangle$ dashed. Pick a potential and an initial state, then press Play. Typed expressions may use any symbols: each new one gets a slider. Change the potential while the clock runs, and the wave function feels it suddenly.

1. A free particle spreads

A narrow packet at rest. Its width grows as $\sigma(t)=\sigma_0\sqrt{1+(\hbar t/2m\sigma_0^2)^2}$: the narrower it starts, the faster it spreads, because a narrow packet is made of a wide range of momenta. Watch $\sigma_x\sigma_p$ in the readout: it starts at its minimum, $\hbar/2$, and grows, while $\sigma_p$ itself never changes (no force).

2. The phase is the momentum

A packet moving to the right has colours cycling along it, at a rate set by $k_0$. Drag the $k_0$ slider through zero: the colour stripes stop, then cycle the other way, and the packet goes left. The momentum panel shows the same thing: $|\tilde\psi(p)|^2$ is centred on $\hbar k_0$.

3. Two packets interfere

Two packets moving towards each other. Where they overlap, $|\psi_1+\psi_2|^2$ has fringes $|\psi_1|^2+|\psi_2|^2+2\,\mathrm{Re}\,\psi_1^*\psi_2$, spaced by $\pi/k_0$. They pass through each other unchanged: the equation is linear.

4. Stationary states, and what moves

The second state of the infinite well. $|\psi|^2$ does not change at all; only the phase turns, as $e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}$ (the colours cycle). Now choose 1 + 2: a superposition of two energies sloshes from side to side with period $2\pi\hbar/(E_2-E_1)$. Only superpositions of different energies move. The bars at the right edge show the state's energy distribution $|c_n|^2$; they never change.

5. Quantum revival

A packet bouncing in a box. It spreads and seems to dissolve into a mess, yet at $T_\text{rev}=4mL^2/\pi\hbar\approx127$ it reassembles exactly, and at fractions of $T_\text{rev}$ it splits into several copies. The reason: the energies $n^2\pi^2/2L^2$ are all whole multiples of one energy, so all the phases line up again. The space-time picture (bottom) draws this as a "quantum carpet".

6. A coherent state: almost classical

A Gaussian with the ground state's width, displaced, in a harmonic well. It swings back and forth without changing shape, with $\langle x\rangle$ (the triangle) following the classical particle (the dot) exactly: for a quadratic potential Ehrenfest's theorem $\frac{d}{dt}\langle p\rangle=-\langle V'(x)\rangle$ is Newton's law. Try the V-shaped potential with the same packet: there $\langle V'\rangle\ne V'(\langle x\rangle)$ and the two part ways.

7. A squeezed state breathes

Narrower than the ground state, this packet's width oscillates at twice the oscillator's frequency, trading position uncertainty for momentum uncertainty and back.

8. Tunnelling

The packet's energy is below the top of the barrier ($\langle E\rangle$ dashed, under the grey), yet part of it gets through. The readout gives the probability transmitted. Widen the barrier: the transmission falls roughly as $e^{-2\kappa a}$, with $\kappa=\sqrt{2m(V_0-E)}/\hbar$. Inside the barrier the wave function decays without oscillating: no colour stripes. $P(x>0)$ in the readout is the probability on the far side: once the packet has gone, it is the transmission.

9. Reflection from a step, even going over it

Above the step, a classical particle would always go on; a wave is partly reflected, with $R=\left(\frac{k-k'}{k+k'}\right)^2$. Below the step, everything comes back, after a short excursion into the forbidden region.

10. Bound states leak into the walls

A finite well has a finite number of bound states (here 3). Each one reaches into the classically forbidden region beyond the walls, decaying as $e^{-\kappa|x|}$: the higher the state, the farther. Click a level (a thin line) to load that state.

11. Tunnelling back and forth

A particle in the left well of a double well is the superposition $(\psi_0+\psi_1)/\sqrt2$ of the two lowest states, which differ in energy by a tiny splitting $\Delta E$. It tunnels to the right well and back with period $2\pi\hbar/\Delta E$. Raise the barrier: the splitting shrinks exponentially, and the tunnelling slows. This is the ammonia molecule's inversion and the qubit's two states.

12. The delta function

An attractive $V=-\alpha\,\delta(x)$ has exactly one bound state, $\psi=\sqrt\alpha\,e^{-\alpha|x|}$ with $E=-\alpha^2/2$. Here the delta is a narrow well of width $w$, so its level sits a little higher, near $-0.46$: the true bound state has a cusp at the delta, made of very short wavelengths, which a well of any width blunts. A packet sent at it is transmitted with probability close to $T=k^2/(k^2+\alpha^2)$: one half for $k=\alpha$ (0.52 here, for the same reason). The slower the packet, the less it can tell the well from a true delta.

13. The quantum bouncer

A ball dropped onto a floor in uniform gravity, $V=gx$. The dot is the classical ball. The quantum one keeps time for a few bounces, then dissolves, and later partly revives. The stationary states are Airy functions.

14. Bloch oscillations

An electron in a crystal ($V_0\cos 2\pi x/a$) pushed by a constant force $F$ does not accelerate forever: it oscillates, with period $2\pi\hbar/Fa$, because its momentum runs to the edge of the Brillouin zone and is Bragg-reflected. Set $F=0$ to see it simply spread. A little of the packet leaks to higher bands each time (Zener tunnelling); a smaller $V_0$ leaks more.

15. Driving at resonance

The ground state of a harmonic oscillator pushed by $F\cos\omega t$. At resonance ($\omega=1$) the packet swings ever wider without changing shape: it is a coherent state whose amplitude grows linearly. Off resonance it beats.

16. A sudden change

The ground state of a narrow box. While it runs, drag the box's width $L$: the walls move and the wave function, unchanged at that instant, is now a superposition of the new box's states (watch the bars at the right). Move the slider slowly instead and it stays in the ground state: the adiabatic theorem.

17. Measurement

Press Position under Measure: a position is chosen at random with probability $|\psi|^2$, and the wave function collapses to a narrow packet there, which then spreads. Press Energy: one stationary state is chosen with probability $|c_n|^2$ and the wave function becomes it, motionless. Measure the energy twice: the same answer.

18. Running time backwards

A spread-out mess, a few seconds after a packet hit a barrier. Press Backwards: the reflected and transmitted parts come back together into the original packet. The Schrödinger equation is time-reversal symmetric (the absorbing walls are not, so they are off here).

How it is computed

The split-operator method: each step multiplies $\psi$ by $e^{-iV\Delta t/2\hbar}$ in position space, by $e^{-i\hbar k^2\Delta t/2m}$ in momentum space (one FFT there and one back), and by $e^{-iV\Delta t/2\hbar}$ again. Every factor has magnitude 1, so probability is conserved exactly, the kinetic energy is exact, and the error per step is of order $\Delta t^3$. The stationary states are found separately, by a finite-difference eigenvalue solver. Walls are infinitely high; ring joins the ends; absorbing edges swallow what reaches them.

Perfectly sharp steps are an idealisation the method pays for with tiny time steps, so a potential with jumps loses its Fourier components above $k=16$ (reflection and tunnelling for $k<5$ are those of the sharp step), and walls taller than 30 are rounded over 0.05. The time step adapts to the potential where the wave function is.
