Experiments

Light of wavelength $\lambda$ passes through an aperture you can paint, and lands on a screen a distance $z$ behind it. The light just past the aperture is a sum of plane waves (its Fourier transform), and each travels on with its own phase $e^{ik_zz}$, $k_z=\sqrt{k^2-k_x^2-k_y^2}$. Close to the aperture the screen shows a sharp shadow with fringes at its edges (Fresnel diffraction); far away, the Fourier transform of the aperture itself (Fraunhofer).

Which you see depends on the Fresnel number $N_F=a^2/\lambda z$ for an aperture of radius $a$. Seen from a point on the screen, paths through the middle and the rim of the aperture differ by about $a^2/2z$, and $N_F$ counts the half wavelengths in that difference: the Fresnel zones the aperture covers. Many zones ($N_F\gg1$): each edge makes its own fringes, about $\sqrt{\lambda z}$ apart, and the shadow is sharp. Less than one ($N_F\ll1$): what is left of the path difference is linear across the aperture, $-xx'/z$, so the screen adds up $e^{-ikxx'/z}$ over it: the Fourier transform of the aperture, a pattern of fixed shape in angle $\theta=x/z$ that grows in proportion to $z$. The handover is around the Fraunhofer distance $a^2/\lambda$. The slider goes to 10 m, several Fraunhofer distances for every aperture here; Far field moves the screen there.

1. One slit, near and far

Slide $z$ from short to long. At first the slit's shadow is sharp, with ripples inside its edges; then the ripples grow and merge; far away ($N_F\ll1$) the pattern settles into the $\mathrm{sinc}^2$ of a slit, wider the narrower the slit, with zeros at angles $\theta=\pm\lambda/a,\pm2\lambda/a\ldots$ for a slit of width $a$. Past 60 mm the screen zooms out with the light, so the pattern keeps its size, and the scale bar gives angles. The slit's 1.4 mm length takes longer: its far field begins near a metre. Far field (10 m) gives the Fraunhofer pattern to within 1%.

2. The edge of a shadow

A straight edge: light reaches into the geometrical shadow a little, and outside it the brightness oscillates, overshooting by 37% at the first fringe. The fringes are spaced as $\sqrt{\lambda z}$: move the screen back and they spread out.

3. The spot of Arago and Poisson

An opaque disk. At the centre of its shadow is a bright spot. Poisson, judging Fresnel's wave theory in 1818, derived this spot to show the theory absurd; Arago looked, and it was there. Every point of the disk's rim is the same distance from the centre, so the waves from the rim all arrive in step.

4. Young's two slits

Two slits a distance $d$ apart: in the far field, fringes spaced by $\lambda/d$ in angle, inside the single slit's envelope. The side view shows them form: two diverging beams, overlapping and interfering.

5. The Talbot carpet

A grating of period $d$ makes a perfect image of itself at $z_T=2d^2/\lambda$ and a shifted one at half that, with finer and finer patterns between. The side view draws this "Talbot carpet", discovered by Henry Fox Talbot in 1836; the same pattern is the quantum revival of a particle in a box, with $z$ for time.

6. A Fresnel zone plate

Rings, open and closed alternately, with radii $r_n=\sqrt{n\lambda f}$: every open ring sends light to the point $z=f$ on the axis in step, so the plate focuses like a lens, to a spot about $0.61\lambda f/R$ in radius for a plate of radius $R$; and also at $f/3$, $f/5$..., from the harmonics of the rings. Watch the side view for the bright foci. The rings stop before they get narrower than 2.5 pixels: drawn on a grid, finer rings would alias, sending light where a real plate doesn't.

7. A lens is a phase

A thin lens changes only the phase of the light, by $-kr^2/2f$: the aperture is drawn in colour, the colour being the phase. At $z=f$ the light gathers into the focus, the Airy pattern of the lens's round opening.

8. White light

Three wavelengths (red, green, blue) at once. Every wavelength diffracts by a different angle, so fringes come out coloured, and a grating spreads its orders into spectra.

9. Speckle

A random aperture, like rough glass or a laser lit wall: the light arriving at each point is a sum of many waves with random phases, and the screen is a random pattern of bright and dark grains, with the size of the far-field pattern of the whole aperture.

How it is computed

The aperture is 512 by 512 pixels of $\Delta x=8$ µm, each the average of the aperture over its area, so that edges and rings fall between pixels smoothly. Such a grid carries angles up to $\lambda/2\Delta x$ (34 mrad in green light): light bent further, by detail finer than a pixel, is lost. Up to $z_c=N\Delta x^2/\lambda$ (60 mm in green) the plane waves travel on that grid, and the screen shows its middle 2 mm. By $z_c$ the steepest of them have crossed half the grid, and on a fixed grid they would come back in at the other side; so from there on the demo uses the Fresnel transform, whose pixels grow in proportion to $z$, as $\lambda z/N\Delta x$. The screen then spans a fixed range of angles, ±17 mrad in green, and zooms out as the light spreads. At $z_c$ the two methods are the same calculation. Past $z_c$ the phase (Field) is shown relative to a spherical wave from the aperture, whose rings would be finer than the pixels, and the side view brightens its rows in proportion to $z^2$, to make up for the spreading.

Light and quantum mechanics

For waves near the axis, $k_z\approx k-(k_x^2+k_y^2)/2k$, and the field obeys $\partial_zU=\frac{i}{2k}\nabla_\perp^2U$: the free Schrödinger equation, with $z$ for time and $1/k$ for $\hbar/m$. Diffraction is the spreading of a wave packet. (Up to $z_c$ this demo uses the exact $k_z$; the Fresnel transform beyond it is paraxial, which at the angles shown changes the intensity by a fraction of a percent.)
