Experiments

Maxwell's equations, solved on a grid of cells in the plane, with everything uniform along $z$, out of the picture. In TM the electric field points out of the picture, and the picture shows $E_z$: red positive, blue negative. In TE it is $H_z$, and $\vec E$ lies in the plane. Lengths are in vacuum wavelengths $\lambda$ of the source, time is in its periods, and $c=1$. Around the picture is a layer that absorbs whatever reaches it, so the waves leave as if into open space.

Drag on the picture to paint with the material chosen under Paint (the eraser takes it off). Drag the source, or the tip of its arrow to aim it. A meter (a green line) measures the power crossing it, in the direction of its little arrow, as a share of the beam's power; drag its ends. The probe records the field at a point.

1. Circular waves

A wire along $z$ carrying an oscillating current sends out circular waves, one $\lambda$ apart. Drag it around. The amplitude falls off as $1/\sqrt r$: in two dimensions the energy spreads over a circle, whose length grows as $r$. Choose Pulse and press Fire for a single ring. Paint some glass in the way: inside it the waves are shorter, $\lambda/n$, and slower.

2. Refraction: Snell's law

A beam from vacuum strikes glass ($n=1.5$) at $30^\circ$. In the glass the waves are shorter, and to keep in step with the waves outside all along the surface they must turn towards the normal: $\sin\theta_1=n\sin\theta_2$. The dashed lines are the directions Snell's law and the law of reflection give; drag the beam's arrow and they follow. A little light is reflected: Fresnel's formula gives 5.8% of the power at $30^\circ$. Turn on Energy flow to see where the energy goes.

3. Total internal reflection

Now the beam is inside the glass and meets the surface at $50^\circ$, beyond the critical angle $\sin\theta_c=1/n$ ($41.8^\circ$). All of it is reflected, yet the field reaches a little way out: the evanescent wave, which runs along the surface and falls off as $e^{-\kappa y}$ with $\kappa=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\sqrt{n^2\sin^2\theta-1}$, by $1/e$ in $0.28\lambda$ here. It carries no energy away. Switch to Intensity to see it. Aim the beam closer to the normal than $41.8^\circ$ and light escapes.

4. Frustrated total internal reflection

Two blocks of glass with a gap of $0.3\lambda$ between them. The beam meets the gap beyond the critical angle, so one surface alone would reflect all of it; but the evanescent wave reaches across the gap and starts a beam on the other side. Meter A reads the power that gets through, 32%; meter B, most of the rest, reflected. Widen the gap and the transmission falls off nearly exponentially, as $e^{-2\kappa d}$, from 84% at $0.1\lambda$ to 10% at $0.5\lambda$: this is tunnelling, as for a particle through a barrier. The notes under the picture give the exact plane-wave answer averaged over the beam's spread of angles; meter A agrees with it to within 1%.

5. Brewster's angle

This is TE: $\vec E$ lies in the picture, in the plane of incidence (p polarisation). At Brewster's angle, $\tan\theta_B=n$ ($56.3^\circ$ for glass), nothing is reflected, and there is no reflected beam to see. Meter A, in the glass, reads 98.8%. Not quite all: a beam is a spread of angles around $56.3^\circ$, and the notes give the 0.8% it should reflect (the last few tenths of a per cent of the beam spread past the meter's ends). The charges in the glass oscillate along $\vec E$, and at this angle that is the direction the reflected beam would take, in which they do not radiate. Switch to TM ($E_z$, s polarisation): the reflected beam appears, and meter A drops to 83.4%.

6. A waveguide and a bend

A strip of glass half a wavelength wide guides light by total internal reflection. A guide this narrow holds a single pattern across it, a mode, with a tail outside it in the vacuum. A beam is sent into its end: meter A finds 84% of it in the guide. Round a bend of radius $2.5\lambda$, meter B finds 75% (wait for it: the light takes about 40 periods to get there). Tighten the bend (the shape's radius): the tail on the outside of the bend would have to go faster than light in vacuum to keep up, and it is shed. At $1\lambda$ meter B reads 40%, at $0.5\lambda$ 19%.

7. A metal waveguide below cutoff

A channel $0.4\lambda$ wide through a metal wall. In TM the electric field runs along the walls and must vanish on them, so across the channel it has to fit at least half a wavelength: nothing narrower than $\lambda/2$ carries a wave. Here the field decays along the channel as $e^{-\kappa x}$. At first a trickle gets out (meter A): switching the source on makes some higher frequencies, above cutoff, which crawl through; then meter A falls below 0.01%. Widen the channel past $0.5\lambda$: about 40% gets through. Switch to TE: now $\vec E$ points across the channel, can be the same all the way across, and passes at any width (17% at $0.4\lambda$; most of the rest is reflected at the entrance).

8. A lens

A wide beam passes a glass lens and comes to a focus; the picture shows the intensity. The cross marks the paraxial focus, $5.4\lambda$ from the lens's centre, from Snell's law at both surfaces for rays close to the axis. The brightest point is nearer, $3.6\lambda$ from the centre: a lens only a few wavelengths across pulls the brightest point of its focus towards itself (the focal shift, a diffraction effect), and the rim of a strong lens focuses shorter than its middle (spherical aberration). The focus is about a wavelength wide.

9. A lens without surfaces

The index can be a formula (under Grid and background; the dotted lines are contours of $n$). Here it is $n=\sqrt{2-(r/R)^2}$ inside a circle of radius $R$, the Luneburg lens. Light bends towards higher index, and this profile brings a parallel beam to a focus on the far edge of the circle. Try the other backgrounds: in a graded-index fibre the light swings back and forth about the axis instead of bouncing.

10. Two slits

A wide beam on a metal wall with two slits $2.5\lambda$ apart. Behind it two circular waves overlap, and they add where their paths differ by a whole number of wavelengths: $\sin\theta=m\lambda/d$, at $23.6^\circ$ and $53.1^\circ$ (dashed). Switch to Intensity. Move the slits closer together and the fringes spread.

11. A photonic crystal

Rods of index 3 on a square lattice $0.37\lambda$ apart. The waves scattered by all the rows add up in step only going back: this frequency lies in a band gap, the light cannot travel in the crystal, and it is reflected. Meter A, behind the crystal, reads almost nothing. Change the lattice constant: the gap of this small crystal runs from about $0.27\lambda$ to $0.45\lambda$, and outside it the light gets through (44% at $0.26\lambda$, 39% at $0.46\lambda$). Electrons in a crystal have band gaps for the same reason.

12. Light round a sharp corner

The same crystal with rods missing: a channel with a right-angled bend. Light at a frequency in the band gap cannot go into the crystal, so it follows the channel, even round a corner that would make a glass waveguide leak.

13. A cavity rings down

A metal box $1/\sqrt2$ wavelengths square inside, whose lowest mode is at the source's frequency, with an opening $0.4\lambda$ wide. A short pulse sets it ringing, and the probe records the field (below the picture) and fits its decay. The quality factor $Q$ is $2\pi$ times the energy stored over the energy lost in a period: the energy falls by $1/e$ in $Q/2\pi$ periods. Here $Q$ is about 85, and the opening pulls the frequency down to 0.93. Narrow the opening to $0.3\lambda$ and $Q$ rises to about 600; close it and the box rings on at 0.9995 (the grid's value for this box) without losing anything.

14. Cherenkov radiation

A source moving through glass at $\beta=0.9$, faster than light travels in glass, $c/n=0.67$. The waves it leaves behind cannot keep up and pile up on a cone, like the bow wave of a boat, and the light goes out at $\cos\theta=1/n\beta$ to the motion ($42.2^\circ$, dashed). Slow it below $\beta=1/n$ and the cone is gone. Switch the moving source to Oscillating for the Doppler effect: the waves crowd together ahead of it and spread out behind.

How it is computed

The finite-difference time-domain method (Yee, 1966): $\vec E$ and $\vec H$ on interleaved grids, stepped alternately, with $c\,\Delta t=\Delta x/2$. It conserves energy exactly, but waves on a grid travel a little slower than they should, more so the fewer cells per wavelength (0.3% slower at 20 cells): the readout warns when there are fewer than 15 cells per wavelength in the highest index. A cell that an interface cuts gets the average permittivity of its parts. At 20 cells per wavelength in the glass, a glass surface reflects within 2% of Fresnel's value ($r=0.203$ for $0.200$). The metal is a perfect conductor, placed to within half a cell. The absorber is a lossy material matched to vacuum: it reflects 0.02% and lets $e^{-12}$ of the power through a wavelength of it. The layer around the picture is a convolutional perfectly matched layer, 16 cells thick, which reflects $10^{-5}$ of a wave at normal incidence. Beams are Gaussian, injected along a line by the total-field / scattered-field method. The meters measure the flow of energy exactly as the scheme conserves it, so their readings add up, and a beam's power is reckoned the same way.
