Experiments

Start here

A radio source is noise: a hiss, like the static between stations. Two antennas far apart hear the same hiss, one of them a little later. Multiply what they hear and average, and the delay comes out as a phase: that one number is what an interferometer measures, and enough of them make a picture.

The page tells this in four numbered views, left to right. 1 · One source: the hiss itself, its spectrum and how long its lumps last (drag the edge of the band). 2 · Two sources, two antennas: drag an antenna along the ground and watch the dot in the complex plane wind round; add a second source and see the two correlations add. 3 · The uv plane: the sky on the left, and on the right the correlation a pair would measure at every separation, in colour; drag the ● and the live dot settles on the colour under it. 4 · Many baselines: a whole array, the turning Earth, the image that comes out, and CLEAN.

Look for this: the correlation of noise is not noisy once averaged, its phase winds with the separation (that is the "fringe"), two independent sources just add, and the map of all the correlations is the Fourier transform of the sky.

1. Band-limited noise: lumps that last 1/Δν

One source, its band 5% of the carrier. The top plot is the voltage it sends, a sine at $\nu_0$ whose amplitude and phase wander; the grey curve is the envelope. The lumps last about $1/\Delta\nu$ = 20 cycles: that is the coherence time. Below left, its spectrum: flat across the band, nothing outside. Below right, its autocorrelation $R(\tau)=\langle x(t)\,x(t+\tau)\rangle$: it oscillates at $\nu_0$ under an envelope that first reaches zero at $\tau=1/\Delta\nu$ = 20 cycles. Drag the band's edge on the spectrum narrower, to 1%: the lumps last 100 cycles and the envelope stretches to match. The envelope is the Fourier transform of the band (Wiener–Khinchin): a flat band of width $\Delta\nu$ gives $\mathrm{sinc}(\Delta\nu\,\tau)$, zero at $1/\Delta\nu$.

2. White noise: no lumps, a spike for an autocorrelation

The band as wide as it goes, from zero to $2\nu_0$: white noise, with no carrier to speak of. Its spectrum is flat everywhere and its autocorrelation is a single spike half a cycle wide at zero lag: each sample is independent of the next. Now drag the band back to 20%: the carrier reappears, with lumps 5 cycles long. A radio source's noise is white; the receiver's band-pass filter is what makes it quasi-monochromatic, and that filtering is what lets two antennas see it as the same wave.

3. Negative frequencies and the complex envelope

The spectrum of a real signal is two-sided: a band at $+\nu_0$ and its mirror image at $-\nu_0$, because $\cos 2\pi\nu_0 t=\tfrac12(e^{2\pi i\nu_0 t}+e^{-2\pi i\nu_0 t})$; the negative-frequency half carries nothing the positive half does not (it is its complex conjugate). Press Complex envelope: the receiver mixes the signal down to baseband, keeping one side only, centred on zero. What is left is the complex envelope $s(t)$, I and Q, two slowly varying curves: the real signal is $x(t)=\mathrm{Re}[s(t)\,e^{2\pi i\nu_0 t}]$. Its spectrum is one band at 0, from $-\Delta\nu/2$ to $+\Delta\nu/2$, and its autocorrelation is the envelope alone, without the oscillation. This is what the correlator works with: a delay $\tau$ of the real signal is the envelope shifted by $\tau$ times a phase $e^{-2\pi i\nu_0\tau}$.

4. One source, two antennas: the fringe is in the separation

A source at $\theta=10°$, antennas A and B 1.0 m apart at 915 MHz ($\lambda$ = 32.8 cm). Their voltages (second row) are the same noise, B's $\tau=D\sin\theta/c$ later (0.53 cycles here: the bracket on the picture). The correlator multiplies A by B's conjugate and averages: the complex plane shows the dot settling, at a magnitude equal to the source's brightness and a phase of $-2\pi\nu_0\tau$ = $-2\pi D\sin\theta/\lambda$, −191°. Drag B away from A: the magnitude stays and the phase winds, one turn for every $\lambda/\sin\theta$ = 1.89 m of separation. That winding is the interference fringe of the handout, $\langle v_1v_2\rangle=I\cos(2\pi\theta D/\lambda)$: a fringe not on a screen but in the separation of the antennas, at the "spatial frequency" $\theta$. Drag the source: the fringe gets finer as $\theta$ grows.

5. Two sources: the correlations add, the cross terms average away

A second source, dimmer, at $\theta=-20°$. Each antenna now hears the sum of two independent noises. The correlator's product has four terms: A's copy of source 1 times B's copy of source 1, likewise for source 2, and two cross terms, source 1 at A times source 2 at B and the reverse. The complex plane draws them: the two self terms as arrows laid end to end, each the fringe of its own source, and the cross terms as the grey dot, which wanders and dies: the two noises have nothing to do with each other, so their product averages to zero. What is left is the sum of the two fringes: two spatial frequencies, and the magnitude beats as you drag B. That is the whole trick: because the sources are incoherent, the correlation of a sky is the sum (the integral) of one fringe per source, $V=\sum_k I_k\,e^{-2\pi i\,u\,l_k}$.

6. A wide band and a long baseline: why the correlator delays one antenna

The source 30° from straight up, the band wide (40%: lumps 2.5 cycles long), the antennas 3 m apart, and the electronic delay switched off. B hears the noise $D\sin\theta/\lambda$ = 4.6 cycles after A: the lumps no longer overlap, and the correlation is dead, not because the sources are incoherent but because A and B are multiplying different stretches of the same noise. Press Delay tracking: the correlator delays A by the geometric delay towards the middle of the sky, the lumps line up and the dot comes back. Narrow the band to 2% instead: the lumps last 50 cycles, longer than the delay, and the correlation survives without tracking. Real arrays point far from the zenith and have delays of microseconds, millions of cycles: without the delay there would be no interferometry, and the delay sets the direction the array "looks" at.

7. The uv plane: drag the pair and read the Fourier transform

The lab's pentagon of five sources. On the right, the correlation a pair would measure at every separation: hue for its phase, lightness for its magnitude (the legend is the complex plane). Its axes are the separation $\Delta x,\Delta y$ in metres along the bottom and $u=\Delta x/\lambda$, $v=\Delta y/\lambda$ in wavelengths along the top: fringes $1/u$ apart on the sky. The ● is where the pair sits now, with its mirror ○ at $(-u,-v)$: swapping A and B conjugates the correlation, so one measurement gives two points. Drag the ●: the live dot in the complex plane (bottom right) settles on the colour under it, the exact value marked by the ring. Every one of the five sources contributes one set of stripes, and they add: the picture is $V(u,v)=\sum_k I_k\,e^{-2\pi i(ul_k+vm_k)}$, the Fourier transform of the sky. Drag a source: its stripes turn and change spacing.

8. A disk: the Airy pattern, and where its first dark ring is

A uniform disk 19° across in place of the points. Its transform is the Airy pattern, rings around a bright centre, the first dark ring at $|b|=1.22\,\lambda/D$: with $D$ as a direction cosine, $1.22/D$ = 3.7 wavelengths = 1.2 m. Drag the ● out along a radius: the dot's magnitude falls to zero there, then comes back with its phase turned by 180° (the hue jumps to the opposite colour) in the next ring. This is how Michelson measured a star's diameter in 1920 and how Hanbury Brown did it again in 1956: find the separation where the fringes vanish. For a picture the live simulation uses 200 points drawn from the brightness, so the dot scatters a few percent about the exact value. Try the ring, the galaxy and Fourier's portrait, and Your picture.

9. The Fourier Etch-a-Sketch

Press Keep what you measure. Each averaged correlation is now painted into the sampled uv plane where the pair is, and at its mirror point, and the image on the left is the inverse transform of everything painted so far. Drag a line through the origin: the image is the sky smeared along the perpendicular (that streak is the dirty beam of your coverage). Draw lines at three angles, then fill in a circle: the sources come apart. This is the optics lab's Etch-a-Sketch, done with the phases kept: there the knobs move a collector in pseudo-thermal light and the correlation of the intensities gives only $|V|^2$ (experiment 18). Here the voltages are correlated and the phase comes free. Hold Shift to lift the pen.

10. The lab bench's near field: the pair's position matters a little

The sources 5 m above the ground, 3 m from the axis, as on the lab bench: no plane waves now, but the exact distances from each source to each antenna. Drag A or B on the ground picture (bottom right) so that the pair keeps its separation but moves sideways: the dot moves a little, although the picture on the right, which is the far-field transform, does not change. In the far field only the separation matters; at 5 m the wavefronts are still curved. This is the notebook's plot of the correlation against ΔX and the mean X.

11. Few antennas, many baselines

A Y of 27 dishes like the Very Large Array: 351 pairs, and so 702 points of the uv plane measured at once (each pair gives its point and the mirror). The second row appears: under the ideal transform, the sampled uv plane, $V$ where a pair measured it and black elsewhere; under the sky, the image you get from the samples, their inverse transform (the dirty image), with the arrow showing the flow. The small panel is the dirty beam, the image one point would give: a six-pointed star of streaks, one per arm. The dirty image is the sky blurred by that beam. Drag a dish and watch its 26 baselines move. Eight dishes on a line, at the marks of a Golomb ruler, give all 28 spacings different.

12. The Earth turns, and the tracks fill the plane

The same Y over 8 hours at declination 40°. As the Earth turns, each pair's point in the uv plane sweeps an ellipse of axis ratio $\sin\delta$ (tracks on the right), the sampled plane fills in, the beam's spokes fade and the image sharpens. Press Turn the Earth to watch it fill. This is Earth-rotation synthesis, Martin Ryle's Nobel prize of 1974. Set the declination to 85°: the tracks are nearly circles; to 5°: nearly lines, and the beam is a streak that can hardly tell north from south.

13. Long baselines see fine detail, short ones the broad shapes

Two point sources 5″ apart at 5 GHz ($\lambda$ = 6 cm), and a Y 2 km across: the finest fringe is $\lambda/B_\mathrm{max}$ = 6.2″, coarser than their separation, and the image shows one blob. Lengthen the arms to 6 km: $\lambda/B$ = 2.1″ and they come apart. The longest baseline sets the resolution, as the diameter of a mirror does. The shortest sets the other limit: a disk larger than $\lambda/B_\mathrm{min}$ has its smooth middle at separations no pair measures, so the dirty image keeps only its edges.

14. An Earth-sized telescope sees a black hole's ring

The nine sites of the Event Horizon Telescope (2018) at 1.3 mm, observing a ring 42 µas across like the one around M87's black hole, over 12 hours. Baselines nearly as long as the Earth is wide, 12,700 km, give $\lambda/B$ = 21 µas, enough to resolve the ring; the slides' round number is 20 µas for 1 mm and 10,000 km. With so few stations the sampled plane is sparse and the dirty beam has strong sidelobes; still, the ring shows, and CLEAN (experiment 15) sharpens it. A track stops where the source sets for one of its stations.

15. CLEAN takes the dirty beam out, point by point

Högbom's CLEAN (1974) supposes the sky is a few points. It finds the brightest pixel of the residual (at first the dirty image), adds a tenth of it to a model there (the loop gain), subtracts a tenth of the dirty beam centred there, and repeats. A binary, two hours of a Y: press Step a few times and watch the residual, then Run. The CLEAN image is the model smoothed by a Gaussian fitted to the beam's main lobe, plus the residual. Smooth emission (the galaxy) takes thousands of components, and the scales no pair measured stay missing.

16. Why this needs incoherent sources

The pentagon again, but Coherent is on: every source now sends the same noise, with a phase step between neighbours, as if one transmitter fed five antennas. The cross terms of experiment 5 no longer average away, and the dot leaves the colour under the ●: the measurement is no longer the Fourier transform of the brightness. Worse, drag A and B together on the ground picture, keeping their separation: the dot moves. With coherent sources the correlation depends on where the pair sits, not only on how far apart it is, so there is no uv plane to map at all. Switch Coherent off: the dot returns, and stays put as the pair slides. Van Cittert and Zernike's theorem, that the correlation of the fields far from an incoherent source is the transform of its brightness, is exactly the statement that the cross terms vanish.

17. Correlate intensities instead: the phase problem

HBT is on: the correlator multiplies the intensities $|v_A|^2$ and $|v_B|^2$ (their fluctuations about the mean) instead of the voltages, as Hanbury Brown and Twiss did in 1956 and as the optics lab's Etch-a-Sketch does with photodiodes. For Gaussian noise $\langle\Delta I_A\,\Delta I_B\rangle=|V|^2$: the dot sits on the real axis, the uv picture shows $|V|^2$ in grey, and the hue is gone. A triangle and the same triangle turned by 180° give the same map (press Triangle, turned): $|V|^2$ cannot tell a source from its mirror image through the origin. That is the phase problem of intensity interferometry and of crystallography; radio arrays escape it by correlating voltages. The scatter is worse too: the dot takes longer to settle.

How it works

Every source $k$ emits its own complex Gaussian noise $s_k(t)$, independent of every other source (incoherent), filtered to a band of width $\Delta\nu$ around the carrier $\nu_0$. The page works with the complex envelope: the real voltage is $x(t)=\mathrm{Re}[s(t)\,e^{2\pi i\nu_0t}]$, so a delay $\tau$ shifts the envelope by $\tau$ and multiplies it by $e^{-2\pi i\nu_0\tau}$. A receiver at $\vec r_j$ hears a source in the direction $\hat s_k$ (direction cosines $l_k$, $m_k$) at the time $\tau_{jk}=-\hat s_k\cdot\vec r_j/c$ (earlier if it is nearer the source), so $$v_j(t)=\sum_k s_k(t-\tau_{jk})+n_j(t),$$ with its own noise $n_j$. On the lab bench's near field the exact distances $R_{jk}$ and amplitudes $z/R_{jk}$ are used instead, as in the lab notebook. The correlator forms, per chunk of 1024 samples (512 cycles), $c=\langle v_A\,v_B^*\rangle$ at zero lag, and a running average $\bar c\leftarrow\bar c+\alpha(c-\bar c)$ as the GNU Radio flowgraph's single-pole filter does. With the baseline $(u,v)=(\vec r_B-\vec r_A)/\lambda$ and sources that are incoherent, the cross terms $\langle s_k s_{k'}^*\rangle$ ($k\ne k'$) average to zero and $$\bar c\to V(u,v)=\sum_k I_k\,e^{-2\pi i(ul_k+vm_k)}\to\int I(l,m)\,e^{-2\pi i(ul+vm)}\,dl\,dm,$$ the van Cittert–Zernike theorem: the correlation of the fields far from an incoherent source is the Fourier transform of its brightness. (The notebook conjugates A instead and gets $e^{+2\pi i(\dots)}$; its image is then the forward transform. Nothing physical changes.) Pictures are drawn with lightness for $|V|$ and hue for its phase, as in the 2D Fourier transform demo. With a band of width $\Delta\nu$ the envelopes at A and B overlap only if their relative delay is within the coherence time, so each source's term carries a factor $\mathrm{sinc}(\Delta\nu\,\Delta\tau_k)$; the correlator's electronic delay removes the delay towards the middle of the sky (and its phase), and what remains smears the edges of a wide field (bandwidth smearing). The small cross in the complex plane is this expected value; the ring is the ideal $V$.

The simulation makes everything in the frequency domain: a white Gaussian process has white Gaussian Fourier coefficients, so each source is a vector of Gaussian bins inside its band, a delay is a phase ramp across them, and the receivers' spectra, the cross-spectrum and the correlations against lag come out with a few transforms per chunk. The envelope is sampled twice per cycle, so "white" fills the band from 0 to $2\nu_0$. For a picture the live simulation uses 200 points drawn from the brightness, with equal shares of the flux; the ideal $V$ on the right is the picture's exact transform. The scatter of one chunk's correlation is the radiometer value $\sigma=\sqrt{P_AP_B/2N}$ with $N=\Delta\nu\,T$ independent samples per chunk.

Many baselines. An array of $n$ dishes gives $n(n-1)/2$ pairs; as the Earth turns a pair's baseline, in coordinates fixed to the sky, sweeps $u=\sin H\,X+\cos H\,Y$, $v=-\sin\delta\cos H\,X+\sin\delta\sin H\,Y+\cos\delta\,Z$ for a source at hour angle $H$ and declination $\delta$: an ellipse of axis ratio $\sin\delta$ (Thompson, Moran and Swenson). The samples are put on the grid of the picture's transform (natural weighting counts each sample, uniform each cell once); the dirty beam is the transform of the sampling alone, the dirty image the transform of the sampled $V$, and the dirty image is the sky convolved with the beam. CLEAN (Högbom, 1974) subtracts the beam point by point and restores with a Gaussian fitted to the beam's main lobe. HBT: for circular Gaussian noise $\langle|v_A|^2|v_B|^2\rangle-\langle|v_A|^2\rangle\langle|v_B|^2\rangle=|\langle v_Av_B^*\rangle|^2$.

Conventions: $l$ east (to the right), $m$ north (up), $u$ and $v$ the same way in the uv plane; sky angles are $\sin\theta$ in degrees on the bench and small angles elsewhere; the units presets change only the labels (and the near field). Astronomers usually draw east on the left.
