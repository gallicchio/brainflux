Experiments

Start here

A radio source sends noise: a hiss, like the static between stations. Two antennas some way apart hear the same hiss, one of them a moment later. Multiply what they hear and average, and that small delay survives as a phase. This one number is what an interferometer measures, and enough of them make a picture of the sky.

The page tells it in four steps, the numbered buttons at the top. 1 · One source: the noise itself, and what multiplying and averaging do to it. 2 · Two sources, two antennas: drag antenna B along the ground and watch the dot in the complex plane wind round. 3 · The uv plane: on the right, the correlation a pair would measure at every separation, in colour; drag the ● and the live dot settles on the colour under it. 4 · Many baselines: a whole array, the turning Earth, and the image that comes out.

Look for this: one antenna alone sees no pattern at all, but the average of the product of two antennas makes fringes as their separation grows; the fringes of different sources add; and the map of every separation is the Fourier transform of the sky.

1 · One source

1. Band-limited noise: lumps that last about 1/Δν

One source of noise, through a band 5% as wide as the carrier ν₀ (Δν = 45 MHz at 915 MHz). The top plot is the voltage it sends: a sine at $\nu_0$ whose size and phase wander, and the grey curve is its envelope. The lumps last about $1/\Delta\nu$ = 20 cycles (22 ns): that is the coherence time. The spectrum below is flat across the band and empty outside it. Drag the band's edge on the spectrum, or the slider, to 1%: the lumps stretch to 100 cycles. The receiver's filter is what makes the hiss "nearly one frequency" (quasi-monochromatic) for that long.

2. Multiply and average: the autocorrelation, one lag at a time

The dashed curve is the same signal a lag $\tau$ later. The middle plot is their product $x(t)\,x(t+\tau)$, blue above zero and red below, and its average is $R(\tau)$, the autocorrelation, the dot on the bottom right curve. At $\tau=0$ the product is $x^2$, never negative, and its average is the power. Here $\tau$ is half a cycle: the copy is upside down, the product is mostly negative, and $R=-0.99$. Drag $\tau$ along: $R$ oscillates at $\nu_0$, a cycle per cycle of lag, under an envelope that first reaches zero at $\tau=1/\Delta\nu$, 20 cycles, where the lumps no longer line up. That envelope is the Fourier transform of the band (the Wiener–Khinchin theorem): a flat band of width $\Delta\nu$ gives $\mathrm{sinc}(\Delta\nu\,\tau)$.

3. White noise: no carrier, no lumps, a spike

The band as wide as it goes, 0 to $2\nu_0$: white noise. Its spectrum is flat everywhere and its autocorrelation is a spike half a cycle wide: each moment is independent of the next. Narrow the band to 20%: the carrier reappears, in lumps 5 cycles long. A radio source's noise is white over a wide range; the receiver's band-pass filter is what lets two antennas see it as the same wave for a while.

4. Negative frequencies and the complex envelope

The spectrum of a real signal has two halves: the band at $+\nu_0$ and its mirror image at $-\nu_0$, because $\cos 2\pi\nu_0t=\tfrac12(e^{2\pi i\nu_0t}+e^{-2\pi i\nu_0t})$. The negative half carries nothing new: it is the complex conjugate of the positive half. Complex envelope is on: the receiver mixes the signal down, keeping one side only and shifting it to zero. What is left is the envelope $s(t)$, two slow curves called I and Q (solid and dashed), with $x(t)=\sqrt2\,\mathrm{Re}[s(t)\,e^{2\pi i\nu_0t}]$. Its spectrum is one band at 0, and its autocorrelation is the envelope without the oscillation. The correlator works with $s$: a delay $\tau$ shifts $s$ by $\tau$ and turns it by the phase $e^{-2\pi i\nu_0\tau}$.

5. Two independent noises: the average of their product is zero

Now the dashed curve is a second source, with its own noise. Their product swings positive and negative with no preference, and however you drag $\tau$, its average dies away (the curve at the bottom right flattens towards zero as the averaging goes on). This is the key to everything after: two sources that have nothing to do with each other add nothing to a correlation but noise that averages away.

2 · Two sources, two antennas

6. One source, two antennas: the fringe is in the separation

A source 10° from straight up, antennas A and B 20 cm apart, at 915 MHz ($\lambda$ = 32.8 cm). The waves of noise come down at 10° and reach B first: B is nearer the source by $D\sin\theta$ (the black bar), 0.106 wavelengths, so B hears everything 0.106 cycles before A. Their voltages (the second row) are the same noise, shifted. The correlator multiplies A by the conjugate of B and averages: the dot in the complex plane settles at the source's brightness, 1, at a phase of $-2\pi D\sin\theta/\lambda$ = −38°. Drag B to the right: the magnitude stays and the phase winds round, once for every $\lambda/\sin\theta$ = 1.89 m. The real part, $\langle x_Ax_B\rangle$, is the handout's $I\cos(2\pi\theta D/\lambda)$: the black curve under the live dot on the fringe plot. That is the interference fringe, not on a screen but in the separation of the antennas. The orange line is the power B receives: the same wherever B is. One antenna alone sees no fringe; only the product of two does. Drag the source: the fringes get finer as $\theta$ grows.

7. Two sources: the correlations add, the cross terms average away

A second source, half as bright, at −25°. Each antenna hears the sum of two independent noises, and the product of A and B has four parts: source 1 at A times source 1 at B, the same for source 2, and two cross terms, source 1 at one antenna times source 2 at the other. The complex plane draws them: each source's own term as an arrow in its colour, laid end to end, and the cross terms as the grey dot, which wanders and dies, as in experiment 5. What is left is the sum of the two fringes, and the black curve beats as you drag B (the slides' "superposition of both sources"). Because the sources are incoherent, the correlation of the sky is a sum of one fringe per source, $V=\sum_k I_k\,e^{-2\pi i\,u\,l_k}$.

8. Coherent sources: fringes at one antenna, and no longer a sum

The same two sources, but one transmitter feeds both: they send the same noise. Now the power at one antenna has fringes (the orange curve): this is Young's double slit, the two waves adding in step at some places and out of step at others, a pattern fixed on the ground. And the correlation is no longer the sum of two fringes: the cross terms stay. Drag A as well: with incoherent sources only the separation matters; here the correlation changes when the pair moves together. Turn Coherent off: the orange line goes flat again.

9. A wide band and a long baseline: why the correlator delays one antenna

The source 30° from straight up, a band of 40% (lumps 2.5 cycles long), the antennas 3 m apart, and delay tracking off. B hears the noise $D\sin\theta/\lambda$ = 4.6 cycles before A: the lumps no longer overlap (look at the two streams) and the correlation is almost gone (0.09), not because anything is incoherent but because A and B are multiplying different stretches of the same noise. The cross-correlation against lag shows where it went: its peak is at the delay. The spectrum shows it too: the phase of A·B* winds across the band, so its parts cancel. Press Delay tracking: the correlator delays B by the geometric delay towards the source (the reference direction), the lumps line up, and the dot comes back to 1. Real arrays have delays of microseconds, millions of cycles: the electronic delay is what sets the direction the array looks at.

3 · The uv plane

10. One baseline measures one set of stripes on the sky

One source, off to one side. The pair's stripes are drawn over the sky: the pattern $e^{-2\pi i(ul+vm)}$, crests a fringe spacing $1/|b|$ apart and across the baseline, coloured by phase. The source sits on one colour, and the live dot settles on that colour: what the pair measures is the colour of the stripe the source sits on. Drag the ● on the uv plane: the stripes turn with the baseline and close up as it grows. For one source $|V|$ is the same everywhere and only the phase winds: the uv plane is a rainbow of straight stripes, $1/l$ apart. Choose real part in the menu to see the lecture's grey stripes.

11. The lab's pentagon: five sets of stripes add up to a Fourier transform

Five sources on a pentagon, as in the lab notebook. Each makes its own stripes in the uv plane, and they add: the picture on the right is $V(u,v)=\sum_kI_k\,e^{-2\pi i(ul_k+vm_k)}$, the two-dimensional Fourier transform of the sky. The thin arrows in the complex plane are the five sources' terms, each in the colour of its stripe, laid end to end; the live dot settles where they end, on the colour under the ●. Its axes: the separation in metres along the bottom and the left, and $u=\Delta x/\lambda$, $v=\Delta y/\lambda$ in wavelengths along the top. The ○ is the mirror point: swapping A and B conjugates the correlation, so one pair gives two points. Drag a source: its stripes turn and change spacing, and the whole pattern changes.

12. A disk: the Airy pattern, and where its first dark ring is

A uniform disk in place of the points. Its transform is the Airy pattern: a bright centre and rings, the first dark ring where the baseline is $1.22/D$ wavelengths for a disk $D$ across. Here $D$ = 9.2°, so that is at 7.6 wavelengths, 2.5 m. Drag the ● out along a radius: the dot's size falls to zero there, then comes back with its phase turned half a turn (the colour jumps to the opposite hue). Michelson measured the diameter of a star this way in 1920: by finding where the fringes vanish. For a picture the live simulation uses 200 points drawn at random from its brightness, so the dot settles on the × (what those points give), a little off the ring (the picture's exact transform). Try the ring, the galaxy and Fourier's portrait, or your own picture.

13. The Fourier Etch-a-Sketch

Keep what you measure is on: every measurement is painted into the sampled uv plane where the pair is, and at its mirror point, and the image on the left is the inverse transform of everything painted so far. Drag one line through the middle of the uv plane: the image is the sky smeared out across your line. Draw lines at three angles, then fill in a disk: the three sources come apart. Press Autopilot to let a spiral fill the plane. This is the optics lab's Fourier Etch-a-Sketch, done with the phases kept: there, two collectors in pseudo-thermal light correlate intensities and only $|V|^2$ comes out (experiment 16).

14. The lab bench's near field: where the pair sits matters a little

The sources 5 m above the antennas and 3 m from the axis, as on the lab bench and in the notebook: the exact distances now, not plane waves. The plot Slide the pair shows the correlation as the pair moves sideways, keeping its separation. In the far field it would be flat; here the wavefronts are still curved and it drifts. Drag on it to move the pair: the dot leaves the colour under the ●, which is the far-field transform. This is the notebook's plot of the correlation against ΔX and the mean X.

15. Why this needs incoherent sources

The pentagon, but every source sends the same noise, with a quarter turn of phase between neighbours, as if one transmitter fed five antennas. The cross terms between sources no longer average away, and the dot leaves the colour under the ●: what the pair measures is not the Fourier transform of the brightness any more. Worse, look at Slide the pair: the correlation now changes as the pair moves with its separation fixed, so there is no uv plane to map at all. Turn Coherent sources off (under More): the curve goes flat and the dot returns to its colour. The van Cittert–Zernike theorem, that the correlation of the fields far from an incoherent source is the Fourier transform of its brightness, is exactly the statement that the cross terms vanish. ("Spatially incoherent sources make coherent fields far away.")

16. Correlate intensities instead: the phase problem

HBT is on: the correlator multiplies the intensities $|v_A|^2$ and $|v_B|^2$ (their swings about the mean) instead of the voltages, as Hanbury Brown and Twiss did in 1956 and as the optics lab's Etch-a-Sketch does with photodiodes. For noise like this $\langle\Delta I_A\,\Delta I_B\rangle=|V|^2$: the dot stays on the real axis, the uv picture shows $|V|^2$ in grey, and the phase is gone. A triangle and the same triangle turned by 180° give the same map (press Triangle, turned): $|V|^2$ cannot tell a sky from its mirror image through the centre. That is the phase problem of intensity interferometry and of crystallography; radio arrays avoid it by correlating voltages.

4 · Many baselines

17. Few antennas, many baselines

The slides' eight antennas on a line, with gaps of 8, 10, 1, 3, 2, 7 and 8 units: their 28 pairs measure every spacing from 1 to 24 units. The second row appears: under the ideal uv plane, the sampled uv plane ($V$ where a pair measures it, black elsewhere); under the sky, the image the samples give, their inverse transform (the dirty image), with the arrow for the flow. The small panel is the dirty beam, the image one point source would give. Pairs on a line measure only one direction of the uv plane, so the image is smeared into streaks across it.

18. A Y of 27 dishes: 351 pairs at once

A Y like the Very Large Array: 27 dishes make $27\cdot26/2$ = 351 pairs, and so 702 points of the uv plane at once, a six-pointed star of them. The dirty beam has six spokes, and the dirty image is the sky blurred by it. Drag a dish on the ground: its 26 baselines move together.

19. The Earth turns, and the tracks fill the plane

The same Y, 8 hours of a source at declination 40°. As the Earth turns, each pair's point in the uv plane sweeps an ellipse of axis ratio $\sin\delta$ (the white tracks); the sampled plane fills in, the beam's spokes fade and the image sharpens. Press Turn the Earth to watch it fill. This is Earth-rotation synthesis (Martin Ryle, Nobel prize 1974). Set the declination to 85°: the tracks are nearly circles; to 5°: nearly straight lines, and the beam is a streak that can hardly tell north from south.

20. Long baselines see fine detail, short ones the broad shapes

Two point sources 5″ apart at 5 GHz ($\lambda$ = 6 cm), and a small Y whose longest baseline is 2.0 km: the finest fringe, $\lambda/B$ = 6.1″, is coarser than their separation, and the image shows one blob. Make the array spread out: at 6 km ($\lambda/B$ = 2.1″) they come apart. The longest baseline sets the resolution, as the diameter of a mirror does: an interferometer is a huge telescope with most of its mirror missing. The shortest baseline sets the other limit: anything larger than $\lambda/B_\mathrm{min}$ lives at separations no pair measures.

21. An Earth-sized telescope sees a black hole's ring

The nine sites of the Event Horizon Telescope (2018), at 1.3 mm, looking at a ring 42 µas across like the one around the black hole in M87, for 12 hours. Baselines nearly as long as the Earth is wide (12,700 km) give $\lambda/B$ = 21 µas, enough to see the ring; the slides' round numbers are 20 µas for 1 mm and 10,000 km. With so few stations the sampled plane is sparse and the dirty beam has strong sidelobes, yet the ring shows. A track stops where the source sets for one of its stations.

22. CLEAN takes the dirty beam out, point by point

Högbom's CLEAN (1974) supposes the sky is a few points. It finds the brightest pixel of the residual (at first the dirty image), adds a tenth of it to a model there (the loop gain), subtracts a tenth of the dirty beam centred there, and repeats. Two sources, two hours of a Y: press Step a few times and watch, then Run. The CLEAN image is the model smoothed by a Gaussian fitted to the beam's main lobe, plus what is left. Smooth emission (the galaxy) takes thousands of points, and what no pair measured stays missing.

How it works

Every source $k$ emits its own complex Gaussian noise $s_k(t)$, independent of every other source (incoherent), filtered to a flat band of width $\Delta\nu$ round the carrier $\nu_0$. The page works with the complex envelope: the real voltage is $x(t)=\sqrt2\,\mathrm{Re}[s(t)\,e^{2\pi i\nu_0t}]$, so that $\langle x^2\rangle=\langle|s|^2\rangle$ is the source's brightness $I$ (the handout's $I=V^2/2$ for a sine of amplitude $V$), and a delay $\tau$ shifts the envelope by $\tau$ and turns it by $e^{-2\pi i\nu_0\tau}$. A receiver at $\vec r_j$ hears a source in the direction $\hat s_k$ (direction cosines $l_k$, $m_k$) at the time $\tau_{jk}=-\hat s_k\cdot\vec r_j/c$, earlier if it is nearer the source: $$v_j(t)=\sum_k s_k(t-\tau_{jk})+n_j(t),$$ with its own noise $n_j$. On the lab bench's near field the exact distances $R_{jk}$ and amplitudes $z/R_{jk}$ are used instead, as in the lab notebook. The correlator forms, for each chunk of 1024 samples (512 cycles of the carrier), $c=\langle v_A\,v_B^*\rangle$, and a running average $\bar c\leftarrow\bar c+\alpha(c-\bar c)$, as the GNU Radio flowgraph's single-pole filter does. Its real part is $\langle x_Ax_B\rangle$, the average of the product of the two real voltages; its imaginary part is the same with B a quarter cycle later. With the baseline $(u,v)=(\vec r_B-\vec r_A)/\lambda$ and incoherent sources, the cross terms $\langle s_k s_{k'}^*\rangle$ ($k\ne k'$) average to zero, and $$\bar c\to V(u,v)=\sum_k I_k\,e^{-2\pi i(ul_k+vm_k)}\to\int I(l,m)\,e^{-2\pi i(ul+vm)}\,dl\,dm,$$ the van Cittert–Zernike theorem. (The notebook conjugates A instead and gets $e^{+2\pi i(\dots)}$; nothing physical changes.) Each source's term is one set of stripes in the uv plane, $1/l_k$ apart; the sum of them is the sky's Fourier transform. The same sum read the other way: one baseline $(u,v)$ weighs the sky with one set of stripes on it, $e^{-2\pi i(ul+vm)}$, crests $1/|b|$ apart.

With a band of width $\Delta\nu$ the envelopes at A and B overlap only if their relative delay is within the coherence time $1/\Delta\nu$, so each source's term carries the band's coherence $\gamma(\Delta\tau)\approx\mathrm{sinc}(\Delta\nu\,\Delta\tau)$, the Fourier transform of the band (Wiener–Khinchin). The correlator's electronic delay removes the delay towards the reference direction; what is left smears the edges of a wide field (bandwidth smearing). In the complex plane the ring is the ideal $V$ and the × what this band and these delays give. The scatter of one chunk's correlation is the radiometer value $\sigma=\sqrt{P_AP_B/2N}$ with $N=\Delta\nu\,T$ independent samples in the chunk. The simulation makes everything in the frequency domain: a white Gaussian process has white Gaussian Fourier coefficients, so each source is a set of Gaussian bins inside its band, a delay is a phase ramp across them, and the spectra and correlations come from the same bins. The time streams on the screen are a slowed-down recording of the same kind of noise, composed with the antennas' delays as they are now; the correlator averages 512 cycles each frame. For a picture the simulation uses 200 points drawn at random from its brightness; the ideal $V$ is the picture's exact transform.

Coherent sources share one noise: then $\langle s_ks_{k'}^*\rangle\ne0$, the power at one antenna has Young's fringes, and the correlation depends on both positions, not only on their difference. HBT: for circular Gaussian noise $\langle|v_A|^2|v_B|^2\rangle-\langle|v_A|^2\rangle\langle|v_B|^2\rangle=|\langle v_Av_B^*\rangle|^2$.

Many baselines. An array of $n$ dishes gives $n(n-1)/2$ pairs. As the Earth turns, a pair's baseline seen from a source at hour angle $H$ and declination $\delta$ sweeps $u=\sin H\,X+\cos H\,Y$, $v=-\sin\delta\cos H\,X+\sin\delta\sin H\,Y+\cos\delta\,Z$: an ellipse of axis ratio $\sin\delta$ (Thompson, Moran and Swenson). The samples go onto the grid of the picture's transform (natural weighting counts each sample, uniform each cell once); the dirty beam is the transform of the sampling alone, the dirty image the transform of the sampled $V$, and the dirty image is the sky convolved with the beam. CLEAN (Högbom, 1974) subtracts the beam point by point and restores with a Gaussian fitted to the beam's main lobe.

Conventions: $l$ east (to the right), $m$ north (up), and $u$, $v$ the same way; on the lab bench sky angles are $\sin\theta$, in degrees, elsewhere small angles. The units presets change the labels (and, for the near field, the geometry). Astronomers usually draw east on the left, as the sky looks from below.
