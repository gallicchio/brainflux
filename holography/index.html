Experiments

A hologram is a photograph of interference. Light from an object, $O$, and a reference beam from the same laser, $R$, fall together on a camera, which records only the brightness $H=|O+R|^2$: the fringes where the two meet. Light the recording with the reference again and it passes on $$HR=(|R|^2+|O|^2)\,R+|R|^2\,O+O^*R^2.$$ The middle term is the object's own wave: look through the plate and the object is there, in depth (the virtual image). The last term is its twin, the conjugate wave, which converges to a real image in front of the plate. The first goes straight on (the zero order).

The camera here has 256 × 256 pixels of 5 µm (a plate 1.28 mm across) and the laser is red, 633 nm. Drag the points in the side and front views. Magnify the hologram to see its fringes, and drag on it to move a viewing window, erase parts of it, or keep just a piece. Its Fourier transform sorts the fringes by direction: fringes of period $\Lambda$ bend light by $\sin\theta=\lambda/\Lambda$. The last view is what a camera looking through the lit plate sees, focused at a distance $d$ you choose: positive behind the plate, where virtual images are, negative in front, for real images. It is drawn in directions, $x/d$, so the picture keeps its size as you focus.

1. One point: a zone plate

One point 30 mm behind the plate, with the reference beam along the axis. The point's light and the reference arrive in step at the middle of the plate, and fall in and out of step farther out. So the hologram is a set of rings, at radii $r_n=\sqrt{n\lambda z}$ (bright for even $n$, dark for odd): a Fresnel zone plate. There are 21 of them out to the edge, ever closer together. A point nearer than $Np^2/\lambda=10$ mm would make rings finer than two pixels.

Lit again, a zone plate is a lens with two focal lengths. It spreads light as if from the point (focus at +30 mm: the dot in the middle of the view), and it also gathers light to a point 30 mm in front (focus at −30 mm, or press on the twin). The red glow round the dot is the light that went straight through, and the twin's light out of focus. In line, everything lies on top of everything else.

2. Gabor's in-line hologram

Three points 80 mm behind the plate, with the reference along the axis, as in Dennis Gabor's first holograms (1948). In focus, the points are sharp dots, but they sit on a bright square: the reference light that went straight through, which is the plate itself out of focus, $L/d$ = 16 mrad wide. On top of that are the twins, which are real images 80 mm in front of the plate, here out of focus over twice that width. No focus gets rid of them. The overlapping twin held holography back until Leith and Upatnieks tilted the reference beam, in 1962.

3. Tilt the reference: the twin moves away

A letter F, 2 mm tall and 120 mm behind the plate, lit through ground glass, with the reference tilted by 0.4°. Raise the angle. The zero order and the twin slide down the view, away from the F, and in the transform the islands move apart. The twin is clear at 0.8°. The light from the F reaches the plate from directions spread over ±14 mrad (the F subtends 17 mrad, and each of its points lights the whole plate, which subtends 11 mrad), and the tilt must exceed that. A haze, the object's light interfering with itself ($|O|^2$), clears at 1.75°. Above 2.85° the fringes become finer than two pixels (experiment 13).

4. Three islands in the transform

The hologram's transform has three islands. The middle one is $|R|^2+|O|^2$: a bright point from the reference alone, and round it the object's light interfering with itself, as wide as the spread of directions between points of the F. Below is the image island, $OR^*$: the object's own spread of directions, moved down by the reference's tilt, $\sin\theta/\lambda$. Above is the twin island, $O^*R$, the same turned half round (the hologram is real, so its transform has $F(-k)=F(k)^*$).

The view shows the same three: the F, the zero order below it, and the twin, which leaves at twice the angle and, being past the largest angle the pixels allow, comes back at the top. Make the object beam stronger (Laser and camera, object/ref.) and the middle island grows brighter.

5. Cut the hologram

Seen through ground glass, every point of the F lights the whole plate, so every part of the plate holds the whole F. Press Cover half: the whole letter is still there, speckled and blurred from side to side, because the plate is now half as wide that way. Then Uncover all and drag out a small square on the hologram. The F survives in any piece, coarser and grainier as the piece shrinks, until the grains are as big as its strokes. A piece of width $D$ resolves directions $\lambda/D$ apart, like a lens of that size. The grains are speckle, from the random phases of the ground glass. (Try the bars too: with half the plate covered, the upright bars blur and the lying ones do not.)

6. Parallax through a window

Three points straight behind one another, 40, 60 and 100 mm behind the plate, seen through a window 0.3 mm wide that sweeps up and down the plate (or drag it yourself on the hologram). The camera is focused at 60 mm. The point at 60 mm stays put; the nearer one slides one way, the farther one the other: parallax, as when you move your head in front of a window. The plate recorded light arriving from every direction it can see, so each window shows the scene from its own position. Moving the window by $y_w$ moves a point at depth $z$ by $y_w(1/d-1/z)$ in this view. A photograph cannot do this.

7. Focus through depth

Points 40, 70 and 120 mm behind the plate, and the focus sweeping back and forth: each point sharpens at its own depth. Out of focus, a point is a small square $L\,|1/d-1/z|$ wide. It is the plate's own outline, just as the blur of an out-of-focus camera has the shape of its aperture. Stop the sweep and press Focus on the image to focus on the selected point.

8. Relight in another colour

The F was recorded in red (633 nm) and is lit with green (532 nm), and it is out of focus. The fringes of a hologram bend light by $\sin\theta=\lambda/\Lambda$, so they bend green light less and the image forms farther away, at $150\times633/532=178$ mm. Press Focus on the image. The F is also smaller, by $532/633=0.84$, and it has moved down towards the zero order, because the reference's fringes also bend green light less. Slide the colour and watch it move. In white light every colour would make its own image, which is why these holograms need a laser to be seen (rainbow and reflection holograms get round it).

9. The conjugate beam: a real image

Light the hologram with the reference reversed, its complex conjugate $R^*$. Now the term $O^*|R|^2$ sends back the conjugate of the object's light: it converges, and makes real images in front of the plate, where a screen would catch them. Focus at −50 mm for the nearer point and −90 mm for the other. The real image is inside out (pseudoscopic): the point that was nearer the plate is still nearer it, so to someone in front, the depths are reversed.

10. A binary hologram

The hologram has been rounded to one bit: each pixel black or white, split at the average brightness (magnified 4 times). The F survives, a little noisier. The fringes are still in the right places, and that is what matters. Computer-generated holograms are often binary: printed, etched, or shown on micromirror displays. Try more bits under Laser and camera: with 4 bits (16 grey levels) the image is all but perfect.

11. A digital hologram of Fourier

Joseph Fourier's portrait, 5 mm across and 150 mm behind the plate, lit through ground glass. Its hologram looks like noise and the transform has the three islands, but through the plate there is Fourier. The engraving has more detail than the hologram can hold. The plate tells apart directions only $\lambda/L$ = 0.5 mrad apart, so the portrait is 67 directions across, and an off-axis hologram can give only part of its directions to the image: the rest go to the zero order and the twin. The grain is speckle again.

12. A lensless Fourier hologram

Here the reference is a point source in the object's own plane, 5 mm below the F, both 150 mm behind the plate. The waves from each point of the F and from the reference curve alike, so together they make straight fringes, and the hologram's transform is a picture of the object: the F turned half round in the image island, and upright in the twin island. One FFT makes the image. X-ray holograms of nanostructures are made this way, with a pinhole beside the sample for the reference.

13. Fringes two pixels apart

The reference is as steep as the pixels allow: fringes $\lambda/\sin\theta=2p$ = 10 µm apart, at $\theta$ = 3.6°. The image island reaches the edge of the transform and comes back on the other side, and the twin, which leaves at twice the angle, comes back right on top of the image. With two pixels per fringe, the camera cannot tell a fringe tilted one way from one tilted the other. Lower the angle until the islands come clear of the edges. The best angle depends on the object, but it is always well short of the limit.

How it is computed

  • The points' light is exact spherical waves, $e^{ikr}$, with $r$ the distance to each pixel. A picture's light reaches the plate by the Fresnel diffraction integral (paraxial, which is accurate at these few degrees), computed by one FFT on a grid in which each sample of the picture is one direction of the view. With the ground glass, every sample has a random phase, so every point of the picture lights the whole plate.
  • The camera records the brightness at the centre of each pixel, without noise; rounding to a few grey levels is optional.
  • The view is a lens focused at $d$ (exact on the axis), then one FFT: the light's directions, $\lambda/Np$ apart, out to $\pm\lambda/2p$. Light at larger angles comes back from the other side, as it does from a real pixelated display. The brightest spots are drawn white, so that fainter light can be seen.
  • The islands' extents (and the note under the transform) come from the paraxial directions of the waves at the corners of the plate. The image positions in the side view come from the paraxial holography equations. The tests check both against the computed images.
