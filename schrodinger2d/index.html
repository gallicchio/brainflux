Experiments

A wave packet, nearly a plane wave across the target, runs into a potential $V(x,y)$ and scatters, with $\hbar=m=1$. The picture is $\psi$ coloured by phase (the colour wheel of the other demos) and lit by $|\psi|$; the potential is drawn faintly, and the dashed circle is the detector. Far away the scattered part is an outgoing circular wave, $$\psi\to e^{ikx}+f(\theta)\,\frac{e^{ikr}}{\sqrt r},\qquad \frac{d\sigma}{d\theta}=|f(\theta)|^2,$$ and in two dimensions a cross section is a length. Everything on the right is measured on the detector circle while the packet goes by.

1. A packet meets a disk

Watch the reflected crescent and the circular wave spreading from the disk. Switch the view to Scattered only: that is $\psi-\psi_\text{inc}$, the difference the disk makes, computed exactly because the free packet's motion is known in closed form.

2. The shadow is the optical theorem

Behind the disk, a shadow. In the Scattered only view the shadow is a forward scattered wave in step against the incident one. The interference between them carries probability away from the forward direction: the table's shadow line is that loss, and it equals the total scattered, line for line. That is the optical theorem, $\sigma=-\sqrt{8\pi/k}\,\mathrm{Re}\,[e^{i\pi/4}f(0)]$: the forward amplitude knows how much is scattered in all directions.

3. The Born approximation

A weak, smooth bump. To first order, $f(\theta)$ is the Fourier transform of the potential at the momentum transfer $q=2k\sin(\theta/2)$: $\frac{d\sigma}{d\theta}=\frac{m^2}{2\pi\hbar^4k}|\tilde V(q)|^2$ (dashed grey). The measured curve follows it. Raise $V_0$: the measured cross section grows less than $V_0^2$ and the Born curve overshoots. Make the bump attractive ($V_0<0$): Born cannot tell the sign; the exact answer can.

4. Scattering puts the wave on a circle

Look at momentum space. The incident packet is a spot at $(k_0,0)$; elastic scattering can only turn $\vec k$, not shorten it, so the scattered wave fills in the circle $|\vec k|=k_0$, brightest where $|f(\theta)|^2$ is largest. The Born approximation says the brightness at $\vec k$ is $|\tilde V(\vec k-\vec k_0)|^2$: the potential's Fourier transform, centred on the incident spot.

5. Partial waves: slow particles scatter in all directions

A small hard disk and a slow packet, $ka\approx1$: only the $m=0$ wave scatters, and the cross section is nearly the same in every direction. The bars show $\sin^2\delta_m$ for each partial wave; $\sigma=\frac4k\sum_m\sin^2\delta_m$. Speed the packet up ($k_0$): more partial waves join in, roughly up to $m\approx ka$, and the scattering turns forward. The dots show $|S_m|=|e^{2i\delta_m}|$, which must be 1 (probability conserved), as a check.

6. A resonance: the packet lingers

An attractive well, with a quasi-bound state near the packet's energy. Part of the wave is caught, circles inside the well, and leaks out long after the rest has gone, in all directions. One partial wave's phase shift passes through $\pi/2$ ($\sin^2\delta_m=1$, the unitarity limit).

7. A screened Coulomb potential

Charge scattering: strongly peaked forward, falling off with angle, as Rutherford found. The screening (length $\ell$) keeps the total cross section finite.

8. Two scatterers interfere

Two disks, a distance $d$ apart across the beam. The waves scattered by each interfere, and $d\sigma/d\theta$ has fringes spaced by about $\lambda/d$: the double slit, done with obstacles instead of holes.

9. The double slit

A wall with two openings. Behind it, two circular waves overlap and make fringes; in momentum space, the transverse momenta $k_y$ are quantised into the diffraction orders. Close one slit ($a$) and watch the fringes go.

10. Bragg scattering from a crystal

A small square lattice of scatterers. The waves from all of them add up only in the directions where $\vec k-\vec k_0$ is a reciprocal lattice vector and $|\vec k|=k_0$: sharp Bragg peaks, where the circle of momentum space crosses the reciprocal lattice (Ewald's construction).

11. The impact parameter

A narrow packet aimed off centre, with impact parameter $b$. Large $b$: it passes by, barely deflected. Small $b$: it bounces off. A classical particle's deflection depends on $b$ alone; a wave's spreads, the more so the narrower the packet.

12. Your own target

Type any $V(x,y)$, using $x$, $y$, $r$ and $\theta$. Sharp edges are softened slightly (an edge sharper than the grid can't be followed in time).

How it is computed

The split-operator method on a $256\times256$ grid, with an absorbing border. On the detector circle (radius $R$) the outward flux of the scattered wave is added up over the collision and divided by the incident fluence: that is the measured $d\sigma/d\theta$, averaged over the packet's spread of energies. At the packet's central energy, the time Fourier transform of $\psi_\text{sc}$ on the circle, expanded in $e^{im\theta}$ and divided by the Hankel functions $H_m(kR)$ and by the incident beam's own partial waves, gives $S_m=e^{2i\delta_m}$ exactly, without assuming $kR\gg1$.
