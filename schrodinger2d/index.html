Experiments

A wave packet, nearly a plane wave across the target, runs into a potential $V(x,y)$ and scatters, with $\hbar=m=1$. The picture is $\psi$ coloured by phase (the colour wheel of the other demos) and lit by $|\psi|$; the potential is drawn faintly, and the dashed circle is the detector. Far away the scattered part is an outgoing circular wave, $$\psi\to e^{ikx}+f(\theta)\,\frac{e^{ikr}}{\sqrt r},\qquad \frac{d\sigma}{d\theta}=|f(\theta)|^2,$$ and in two dimensions a cross section is a length. Everything on the right is measured on the detector circle while the packet goes by. A target that isn't round can be turned (turn): the same as sending the packet in from another direction, with $\theta$ still the scattering angle.

1. A packet meets a disk

Watch the reflected crescent and the circular wave spreading from the disk. Switch the view to Scattered only: that is $\psi-\psi_\text{inc}$, the difference the disk makes, computed exactly because the free packet's motion is known in closed form.

2. The shadow is the optical theorem

Behind the disk, a shadow. In the Scattered only view the shadow is a forward scattered wave in step against the incident one. The interference between them carries probability away from the forward direction: the table's shadow line is that loss, and it equals the total scattered, line for line. That is the optical theorem, $\sigma=-\sqrt{8\pi/k}\,\mathrm{Re}\,[e^{i\pi/4}f(0)]$: the forward amplitude knows how much is scattered in all directions.

3. The Born approximation

A smooth bump, $V_0=1$, half the packet's energy: about a sixth of the packet is scattered, most of it within $60^\circ$ of forward. To first order, $f(\theta)$ is the Fourier transform of the potential at the momentum transfer $q=2k\sin(\theta/2)$: $\frac{d\sigma}{d\theta}=\frac{m^2}{2\pi\hbar^4k}|\tilde V(q)|^2$ (dashed grey). The measured curve follows it over the forward peak, and Born's total is only 7% too high. Lower $V_0$ to 0.3 and they agree to 1%, but then less than 2% of the packet scatters: that is what weak means. Raise $V_0$ to 2: the measured cross section grows less than $V_0^2$, and Born is 60% too high. Make the bump attractive ($V_0=-1$): Born cannot tell the sign; the exact answer can (2.49 instead of 2.66).

4. Scattering puts the wave on a circle

The same bump, in the Scattered only view, so that momentum space shows $\psi-\psi_\text{inc}$ alone. The incident packet is a spot at $(k_0,0)$; elastic scattering can only turn $\vec k$, not shorten it, so the scattered wave fills in the circle $|\vec k|=k_0$, brightest where $|f(\theta)|^2$ is largest. The Born approximation says the brightness at $\vec k$ is $|\tilde V(\vec k-\vec k_0)|^2$: the potential's Fourier transform, centred on the incident spot.

5. Partial waves: slow particles scatter in all directions

A small hard disk and a slow packet, $ka\approx1$, in a bigger box (slow waves are long): only the $m=0$ wave scatters, and the cross section is nearly the same in every direction. The bars show $\sin^2\delta_m$ for each partial wave; $\sigma=\frac4k\sum_m\sin^2\delta_m$. Speed the packet up ($k_0$): more partial waves join in, roughly up to $m\approx ka$, and the scattering turns forward. The dots show $|S_m|=|e^{2i\delta_m}|$, which must be 1 (probability conserved), as a check.

6. A resonance: the packet lingers

An attractive well, with a quasi-bound state near the packet's energy. Part of the wave is caught, circles inside the well, and leaks out long after the rest has gone, in all directions. One partial wave's phase shift passes through $\pi/2$ ($\sin^2\delta_m=1$, the unitarity limit).

7. A screened Coulomb potential

Charge scattering: strongly peaked forward, falling off with angle, as Rutherford found. The screening (length $\ell$) keeps the total cross section finite.

8. Two scatterers interfere

Two disks, a distance $d$ apart across the beam. The waves scattered by each interfere, and $d\sigma/d\theta$ has fringes spaced by about $\lambda/d$: the double slit, done with obstacles instead of holes.

9. The double slit

A wall with two openings, $a=1.2$ wide and $d=5$ apart. Behind it, two circular waves overlap and make fringes. There is no incident wave behind a wall to subtract, so the graph shows the flux of $\psi$ itself through the far half of the detector, from $-90^\circ$ to $90^\circ$: every direction there is, since $\sin\theta=k_y/k$ runs from $-1$ to $1$. The dashed curve is Fraunhofer's, for a plane wave $$\frac{d\sigma}{d\theta}\propto\cos^2\theta\,\cos^2\Big(\frac{kd\sin\theta}{2}\Big)\Big(\frac{\sin(ka\sin\theta/2)}{ka\sin\theta/2}\Big)^2,$$ with the packet's spread of energies: dark fringes where $d\sin\theta=(n+\frac12)\lambda$, here $\pm18^\circ$ and $\pm70^\circ$, and bright ones between, near $d\sin\theta=n\lambda$ ($0$ and $\pm39^\circ$; the falling envelope pulls them in a little). The two are scaled to the same height: how much gets through slits about a wavelength wide with soft edges is beyond Fraunhofer (the table has it), but where it goes is not. Only within a degree or so of $\pm90^\circ$, where the detector runs into the wall (shaded), is nothing measured. In momentum space the transverse momenta $k_y$ are the diffraction orders. Move the slits apart ($d$) and the fringes close up; make them narrower ($a$) and the envelope spreads.

10. Bragg reflection from a crystal

A square crystal of $7\times7$ atoms, $c=2$ apart, turned by $\alpha=30^\circ$, in a beam of wavelength $\lambda=2$. Each row of atoms reflects a little of the wave, like a half-silvered mirror, and lets the rest through; the waves reflected by successive rows add up only if their paths differ by whole wavelengths, $2c\sin\alpha=n\lambda$ (Bragg). Here $2c\sin30^\circ=\lambda$: a strong beam leaves at $2\alpha=60^\circ$, about a quarter of the packet (the table). In momentum space the circles are $\vec k_0+\vec G$: the reciprocal lattice, $\vec G=\frac{2\pi}{c}(n_1,n_2)$ turned with the crystal, hung on the incident spot. A scattered wave $\vec k_0+\vec G$ must also be on the energy circle $|\vec k|=k_0$; where a lattice point sits on it (filled), there is a Bragg beam: Ewald's construction. Born's curve is the crystal's Fourier transform, the kinematic theory of diffraction.

11. Off the Bragg angle

The same crystal turned to $\alpha=20^\circ$: $2c\sin\alpha/\lambda=0.68$, no lattice point on the energy circle, and the beam mostly goes through; a sixth as much goes to $2\alpha$. Turn the crystal slowly back to $30^\circ$ and the reflected beam comes back, strong only within a few degrees of the Bragg angle (the crystal is small, so its lattice points are not sharp). Or keep $\alpha=20^\circ$ and shorten the wavelength until $2c\sin\alpha=\lambda$: $k_0=4.6$, and the beam reflects to $40^\circ$. At $\alpha=60^\circ$ it is the columns that reflect, $2c\cos\alpha=\lambda$, to $-60^\circ$.

12. The impact parameter

A narrow packet aimed off centre, with impact parameter $b$. Large $b$: it passes by, barely deflected. Small $b$: it bounces off. A classical particle's deflection depends on $b$ alone; a wave's spreads, the more so the narrower the packet.

13. Your own target

Type any $V(x,y)$, using $x$, $y$, $r$ and $\theta$. Sharp edges are softened slightly (an edge sharper than the grid can't be followed in time).

How it is computed

The split-operator method on a $256\times256$ grid, with an absorbing border. On the detector circle (radius $R$) the outward flux of the scattered wave is added up over the collision and divided by the incident fluence: that is the measured $d\sigma/d\theta$, averaged over the packet's spread of energies. At the packet's central energy, the time Fourier transform of $\psi_\text{sc}$ on the circle, expanded in $e^{im\theta}$ and divided by the Hankel functions $H_m(kR)$ and by the incident beam's own partial waves, gives $S_m=e^{2i\delta_m}$ exactly, without assuming $kR\gg1$ (for a round target: one that isn't round mixes the partial waves, and has no phase shifts). Behind a wall, the flux of $\psi$ itself is measured instead of the scattered flux.
