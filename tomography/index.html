Experiments

A CT scanner sends a sheet of parallel X-rays through one slice of the body and measures, with a row of detectors, how much each ray has been absorbed. That is the integral of the attenuation $f(x,y)$ along the ray, $$p(\theta,s)=\int f\,dl\quad\text{along the line}\quad x\cos\theta+y\sin\theta=s,$$ for the ray at distance $s$ from the centre, crossing the direction $\hat n=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$. Turning source and detector through half a turn fills the sinogram, one row per angle. The task is to get $f$ back.

Back-projection smears every projection back along its rays and adds them up, $b(\vec r)=\int_0^\pi p(\theta,\vec r\cdot\hat n)\,d\theta$: a blurred picture. Filtered back-projection first convolves each projection with the ramp filter, whose Fourier transform is $|k|$, and then smears it back: that gives $f$ itself.

Point at the object to see its sinusoid in the sinogram; click the sinogram to see the ray it measured. Values are attenuation in units of water's; the scan circle is 20 cm across.

1. One point, one sinusoid

A single small object, off centre. As the scanner turns, its shadow on the detector moves to and fro: at angle $\theta$ it falls at $s=x_0\cos\theta+y_0\sin\theta=r_0\cos(\theta-\phi_0)$. So its row-by-row record is a sinusoid, whose amplitude is the point's distance $r_0$ from the centre and whose phase is its direction $\phi_0$. This is where the name sinogram comes from. Move the point with its sliders, or point anywhere on the scanner: the dashed curve is that point's sinusoid. Every object is a sum of points, so its sinogram is a sum of sinusoids.

2. One projection, smeared back

With a single angle, back-projection can only spread the projection evenly along the direction the rays came from: stripes. The scanner cannot tell where along a ray the absorption happened. Raise the number of angles to 2, 3, 4, 8: the stripes cross, and they add up most where the object is.

3. Back-projection blurs a point into 1/r

One point, many angles. Every smear passes through the point, so the back-projection is a star of lines that merge into a halo falling off as $1/r$: each line contributes in a band of width $ds$, and the angle it subtends at distance $r$ is $ds/r$. The profile (the sixth panel) shows the back-projection through the point against $m/r$, for the point's mass $m$, with no adjustable factor. Every point of an object is blurred this way, which is why the back-projection of the head looks foggy.

4. The ramp filter sharpens

Before smearing, each projection is convolved with the ramp filter. The sixth panel shows one projection (grey) and its filtered version (blue): the filter is a sharpener, it overshoots at every edge and goes negative beside it. Smeared back, those negative side lobes cancel the $1/r$ halos of the neighbouring lines, and the picture comes out sharp, with the right values: compare the profiles of the object and the reconstruction.

5. The projection-slice theorem

The 1D Fourier transform of a projection is a slice through the 2D Fourier transform of the object: $$P(\theta,k)=F(k\cos\theta,\,k\sin\theta),$$ along the line through $k=0$ in the direction $\hat n$, across the rays. Here the object is a disk with stripes, which put two bright spots in $F$ at $\pm\vec k$ (the object's own transform is drawn faintly underneath). Watch the slices fill Fourier space: the spots light up only when the slice passes through them, when the rays run along the stripes. Collect slices at enough angles and you have $F$, and so the object.

6. Why the filter is |k|

A few angles, so the slices are easy to count. Each slice has the same number of samples, so near the centre of Fourier space they crowd together and far out they spread apart: at radius $k$ the slices are $k\,\Delta\theta$ apart, so the density of samples falls as $1/|k|$. Back-projection adds every sample with the same weight, which over-counts the low frequencies by $1/|k|$; in real space that is the $1/r$ blur (in 2D, $1/r$ and $1/|k|$ are each other's Fourier transforms). Weighting each slice by $|k|$ undoes it: the ramp filter. Choose Direct Fourier in the sixth panel for the other way to use the theorem: interpolate the slices onto a square grid and take one inverse 2D FFT. It works, but interpolating far from the centre, where the slices are sparse, is hard, and the errors spread over the whole picture.

8. A limited range of angles: the missing wedge

The scanner turns through only 110° instead of 180°. The slices leave a wedge of Fourier space empty, and with it every detail that varies in those directions. Edges are seen by the rays that run along them; edges no ray ran along are lost, so the picture smears in one direction. (Electron tomography of a thin sample, which cannot be tilted beyond about 70°, has exactly this problem.) Widen the range and the wedge closes.

9. Noise: the ramp amplifies it

Each ray now counts photons: $n$ arrive out of $I_0$ sent, Poisson distributed, and the scanner reports $-\ln(n/I_0)$. Few photons through the middle of the head means noisy rays there. The noise is spread evenly over all frequencies, and the ramp multiplies the high frequencies by $|k|$: the reconstruction is grainy. Choose Hann: the window rolls the filter smoothly to zero at the cutoff, the grain drops to about a third (read the RMS difference under the picture), and edges blur a little. Lower the cutoff to trade more. The noise falls as $1/\sqrt{I_0}$: try $I_0$ ten times larger, and the dose ten times larger with it.

10. Detector sampling and aliasing

Only 64 detector bins, each narrow (a fifth of the spacing between them), measuring the sharp-edged head. The edges hold detail finer than the bins can sample, and it comes back at the wrong frequencies: fine ripples and streaks tangent to the skull. Widen the detectors (the slider under Detector width) to 1: each bin now averages over its width, a low-pass filter before sampling, and the aliasing mostly goes, at the price of a little blur. More bins sharpen the picture and push aliasing to finer scales.

11. Metal makes streaks

Two metal pins, twenty times as absorbing as water. For an ideal scanner they are no problem: turn the noise off and the beam monochromatic, and the reconstruction is clean. Real scanners have two problems with them. Few photons get through the metal (photon starvation), so those rays are very noisy, and filtered back-projection spreads their errors along the rays as streaks. And the beam has many energies; the soft photons are absorbed first, so long paths through metal look less absorbing than they are (beam hardening), which leaves a dark band between the pins. Turn each effect on and off.

12. The faint features of the Shepp–Logan phantom

The phantom of Shepp and Logan (1974) has a skull of attenuation 2 and a brain of 1.02, and its small ellipses differ from the brain by 1%. A grey scale from 1.0 to 1.05 shows them, as CT viewers use narrow "windows" for soft tissue. Now the faint features compete with noise: lower the photon count and see when the three small spots at the bottom can no longer be told apart, and how much the Hann window helps.

Things to know

  • Parallel beams: half a turn measures every line once, since $p(\theta+\pi,s)=p(\theta,-s)$. Real scanners use a fan of rays from one point and turn all the way round.
  • Filtered back-projection here: $f(\vec r)=\sum_j Q(\theta_j,\vec r\cdot\hat n_j)\,\Delta\theta$, with $Q$ the projection convolved with the band-limited ramp kernel (Kak and Slaney, 1988), interpolated linearly between detector bins.
  • The RMS difference compares the reconstruction with the object, pixel by pixel, inside 95% of the scan circle.
  • Photon counting is exact (Poisson); the two-energy beam is a simple model of beam hardening in which every material absorbs the soft photons twice as strongly as the hard ones. No correction is applied for it.
