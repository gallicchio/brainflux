Experiments

A CT scanner sends a sheet of parallel X-rays through one slice of the body and measures, with a row of detectors, how much each ray has been absorbed. That is the integral of the attenuation $f(x,y)$ along the ray, the line $x\cos\theta+y\sin\theta=s$: $$p(\theta,s)=\int_{\mathrm{ray}} f\,dl,$$ for the ray at distance $s$ from the centre, across the direction $\hat n=(\cos\theta,\sin\theta)$. Turning source and detector through half a turn fills the sinogram, one row per angle. The task is to get $f$ back.

Back-projection smears every projection back along its rays and adds them up, $b(\vec r)=\int_0^\pi p(\theta,\vec r\cdot\hat n)\,d\theta$: a blurred picture. Filtered back-projection first convolves each projection with the ramp filter, whose Fourier transform is $|k|$, and then smears it back: that gives $f$ itself.

Point at the object to see its sinusoid in the sinogram; click the sinogram to see the ray it measured, on the object and in the back-projection. Values are attenuations in units of water's, and the scan circle is 20 cm across.

1. One point, one sinusoid

A single small object, off centre (it is one pixel wide: the ring shows where). As the scanner turns, its shadow on the detector moves to and fro, as the sixth panel shows: at angle $\theta$ it falls at $s=x_0\cos\theta+y_0\sin\theta=r_0\cos(\theta-\phi_0)$. So its record, row by row, is a sinusoid whose amplitude is the point's distance $r_0$ from the centre and whose phase is its direction $\phi_0$; hence the name sinogram. Move the point with its sliders, or point anywhere on the scanner: the dashed curve is that point's sinusoid. Every object is a sum of points, so its sinogram is a sum of sinusoids.

2. One projection, smeared back

With a single angle, back-projection can only spread the projection evenly along the direction the rays came from: stripes. The scanner cannot tell where along a ray the absorption happened. Raise the number of angles to 2, 3, 4, 8: the stripes cross, and they add up most where the object is.

3. Back-projection blurs a point into 1/r

One point, 360 angles. Every smear passes through the point, so the back-projection is a star of lines that merge into a halo. A place at distance $r$ from the point lies within a detector bin $ds$ of the smears from a fraction $ds/\pi r$ of the angles, so the halo falls off as $1/r$: exactly, $b=m/r$ for a point of mass $m$. The profile (the sixth panel) shows the back-projection along the line through the point (orange, right-hand scale) and $m/r$ (dotted), with nothing adjusted: they lie on top of each other. Every point of an object is blurred this way, which is why the back-projection of the head looks foggy.

4. The ramp filter sharpens

Before smearing, each projection is convolved with the ramp filter. The sixth panel shows one projection (grey) and its filtered version (blue): the filter is a sharpener, it overshoots at every edge and goes negative beside it. Smeared back, those negative side lobes cancel the $1/r$ halos of the neighbouring lines, and the picture comes out sharp, with the right values. Click the sinogram to see other projections, and choose Profile to compare the reconstruction with the object along a line.

5. The projection-slice theorem

The 1D Fourier transform of a projection is a slice through the 2D Fourier transform of the object: $$P(\theta,k)=F(k\cos\theta,\,k\sin\theta),$$ along the line through $k=0$ in the direction $\hat n$, across the rays. Here the object is a disk with stripes; their wavevector puts two bright spots in $F$, at $|k|=40$ in the direction 60°. Fourier space is magnified four times, with the object's own transform drawn faintly underneath. Watch the slices arrive, one every 5°: the spots light up when the slice reaches 60°, when the rays run along the stripes. Collect slices at enough angles and you have $F$, and so the object.

6. Why the filter is |k|

Twelve angles, so the slices are easy to count. Each slice has the same number of samples, so near the centre of Fourier space they crowd together and far out they spread apart: at radius $k$ the slices are $k\,\Delta\theta$ apart, and the density of samples falls as $1/|k|$. Back-projection adds every sample with the same weight, which over-counts the low frequencies by $1/|k|$; in real space that is the $1/r$ blur (in 2D, $1/r$ and $1/|k|$ are each other's Fourier transforms, up to $2\pi$). Weighting each slice by $|k|$ undoes it: the ramp filter.

The sixth panel uses the theorem directly: it interpolates the slices onto a square grid and takes one inverse 2D FFT. With these twelve slices its RMS error is 0.12, against 0.26 for filtered back-projection, whose streaks cost more; with 180 angles it does worse, 0.034 against 0.023, because interpolating far from the centre, where the slices are sparse, is hard, and an error at one $k$ spreads over the whole picture.

7. Too few angles: streaks

The head with 24 angles. Close to each sharp edge the smears still cancel, but further away they separate into streaks. Fourier space shows why: the slices are $k\,\pi/n_\theta$ apart at radius $k$, and to describe an object of radius $R$ (here the scan circle, 1) samples must be at most $\pi/R$ apart. Inside the dotted circle, $|k|<\pi/\Delta\theta=n_\theta/R$, they are; outside they are not, though the detector measures out to the dashed circle, $|k|=\pi/ds$. The circles meet when $n_\theta=\tfrac{\pi}{2}M$ for $M$ detector bins: the number of angles that samples the whole scan circle fully. Raise the angles and watch the streaks retreat: the RMS error away from edges (under the picture) falls from 0.15 at 24 angles to 0.067 at 48, 0.023 at 96 and 0.009 at 180; beyond that the streaks are faint and little more is gained (0.008 at $\tfrac{\pi}{2}\times256\approx400$).

8. A limited range of angles: the missing wedge

The scanner turns through only 110° instead of 180°. The slices leave a wedge of Fourier space empty, and with it every detail that varies in those directions. An edge is seen by the rays that run along it; edges no ray ran along are lost, so the picture smears in one direction. (Electron tomography of a thin slab, which cannot be tilted much beyond 60° to 70° either way, has exactly this problem.) Widen the range and the wedge closes. The scan building up shows the same thing as it goes: until it has turned through 180° every reconstruction is a limited-angle one.

9. Noise: the ramp amplifies it

Each ray now counts photons: $n$ arrive out of $I_0$ sent, Poisson distributed, and the scanner reports $-\ln(n/I_0)$. Along the longest paths through the 1974 head (realistic attenuations) only 2% of the photons get through, so those rays are noisy. The noise is spread evenly over all frequencies, and the ramp multiplies the high frequencies by $|k|$: the picture is grainy, and the ventricles (2% darker than the brain) are lost. Choose Hann: the window rolls the filter smoothly to zero at the cutoff, the grain drops to about a third (the RMS error away from edges, under the picture, goes from 0.043 to 0.015), the ventricles reappear, and edges blur a little. Lower the cutoff to trade more. With Ram-Lak, ten times the photons (ten times the dose) cut the noise by $\sqrt{10}$; the reading falls from 0.043 to 0.020, not 0.014, because 0.016 of it is streaks and ringing that are there without noise (turn the noise off to see).

10. Detector sampling and aliasing

A zone plate, $\cos(kr^2)$, whose rings get finer outwards (the local wavenumber is $2kr$), scanned by only 64 detector bins, each a fifth of the spacing wide. 64 bins sample up to $|k|=\pi/ds=100$, which the rings pass at $r=0.5$. Beyond that, the reconstruction shows rings that are not there, coarser and in the wrong places: detail finer than the sampling comes back at the wrong frequency (aliasing). Under Detector width, widen the bins to 1: each now averages over its width, which removes much of that detail before it is sampled, and the false rings fade to grey. Then raise the bins to 256: every ring is there.

11. Metal makes streaks

Two metal pins, twenty times as absorbing as water, in the 1974 head, reconstructed with the Hann filter as soft tissue is. Turn off the noise and choose one energy: an ideal scanner gets the brain between the pins right, with only faint ripples round the pins (their edges are too sharp and bright for 180 angles and 256 bins to capture fully). A real scanner adds two worse problems. Few photons get through the metal (photon starvation), so the rays through it are very noisy, and filtered back-projection spreads their errors along the rays: streaks fanning out from the pins. And the beam has many energies. The scanner corrects for the hardening of the beam as water causes it, but metal absorbs the soft photons far more strongly than the hard ones, so rays through metal look less absorbing than they are: a dark band joins the pins, where rays pass through both, and the brain there comes out about 0.87 instead of 1.02. Turn each effect on and off.

12. The faint features of the Shepp–Logan phantom

Shepp and Logan's phantom of 1974 has a skull of attenuation 2 and a brain of 1.02, and its small ellipses differ from the brain by 1%. The grey scale runs from 1.00 to 1.05, as CT viewers use narrow "windows" for soft tissue; the skull is far off the scale. Turn on photon noise at $10^6$ photons: the three small spots at the bottom disappear in the grain. Choose Hann and they come back, a little blurred; or keep Ram-Lak and send $10^7$ photons.

13. Your own picture, or the camera

Fourier's portrait, scanned at 360 angles. Paint on it with the brush (the reconstruction follows), or choose Your picture… or Camera. With the camera the scanner keeps turning and each angle sees the picture of its moment: move during the scan and see what motion does to a CT image. (Pictures are projected numerically, by summing along each ray with linear interpolation, accurate to a fraction of a percent; the phantom, the point and the disk exactly.)

Things to know

  • Parallel beams: half a turn measures every line once, since $p(\theta+\pi,s)=p(\theta,-s)$. Real scanners use a fan of rays from one point and turn all the way round.
  • Filtered back-projection here is $f(\vec r)=\sum_j Q(\theta_j,\vec r\cdot\hat n_j)\,\Delta\theta$, with $Q$ the projection convolved with the band-limited ramp kernel (Kak and Slaney, 1988), interpolated linearly between detector bins. Each detector bin averages the projection over its width.
  • The RMS error compares the reconstruction with the object pixel by pixel, inside 95% of the scan circle; "away from edges" counts only pixels whose surroundings (four pixels each way) are uniform in the object, so that it measures noise and streaks rather than blurred edges.
  • Photon counting is exact (Poisson). The two-energy beam is a simple model of beam hardening: water-like matter absorbs the soft photons 1.35 times as strongly as the hard ones, metal 4 times; the scanner corrects exactly for water, not for metal.
  • The scanner measures the object as it is at each angle, so changing the object during a scan (painting, sliders, the camera) gives the motion artefacts of a real scan. Once a scan is over, any change measures every angle again at once.
