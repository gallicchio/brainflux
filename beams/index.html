Experiments

A laser beam is a wave that stays narrow: a Gaussian beam. Its radius $w(z)$, where the intensity has fallen to $1/e^2$ (13.5%) of its value on the axis, is smallest at the waist $w_0$, and $\sqrt2$ times that one Rayleigh range $z_R=\pi w_0^2/\lambda$ away. Far beyond, the beam spreads in a cone of half-angle $\theta=\lambda/\pi w_0$. With $z$ measured from the waist, $$w(z)=w_0\sqrt{1+z^2/z_R^2},\qquad R(z)=z\left(1+z_R^2/z^2\right),$$ where $R$ is the radius of curvature of the wavefronts. All of this is held in one complex number, the beam parameter $q=z+iz_R$, or $$\frac1q=\frac1R-i\frac{\lambda}{\pi w^2}.$$ A lens, a mirror, a slab of glass or a stretch of empty space changes $q$ by the ABCD law $q'=(Aq+B)/(Cq+D)$, with the elements $A,B,C,D$ of its ray-transfer matrix, the same as for rays ($B=d$ for a distance $d$, $C=-1/f$ for a lens).

Three views: the Bench, a beam through lenses, mirrors and glass; the Cavity, two mirrors that trap light; and the Modes, the beams that keep their shape. Everything is paraxial (small angles: $w_0\gg\lambda$), and lenses and mirrors are thin.

1. A beam and a lens

The picture the page opens with. A red helium–neon laser beam ($\lambda=633$ nm) has its waist $w_0=200$ µm at $z=0$. One Rayleigh range away, at $z=\pm z_R=\pm199$ mm (the dashed lines), it is $\sqrt2$ times wider; far away it spreads at $\theta=\lambda/\pi w_0=1.0$ mrad (the dotted cone). At $z=400$ mm a lens of $f=200$ mm catches it, 450 µm wide and still spreading, and makes a new waist of 142 µm at $z=701$ mm, with $z_R'=100$ mm: a waist about $1/\sqrt2$ as wide has about half the Rayleigh range, as $z_R\propto w_0^2$.

Drag the lens and watch the new waist move and change size. Drag the yellow ring down to make the input waist smaller: the beam spreads faster. Ray optics would image the input waist, $2f$ before the lens, $2f$ after it and the same size; why the beam's waist comes out at 301 mm and 0.71 times as wide is experiment 6.

2. The beam alone: its width and its wavefronts

The same beam without the lens, with its wavefronts: arcs of radius $R(z)$, as measured on the $z$ axis. They are flat at the waist, most curved at $z=\pm z_R$, where $R=2z_R=397$ mm, and flatter again far away, where $R\approx z$: a spherical wave coming from the waist.

Type $w_0=100$ µm. The Rayleigh range falls four times, to 49.6 mm, and $\theta$ doubles, to 2.01 mrad: a narrower beam spreads faster, $\theta=\lambda/\pi w_0$, just as light through a narrower slit diffracts more. Then press Crests to see the wave itself: its crests have the wavefronts' shape.

3. Focusing: a wider beam makes a smaller spot

A beam 1 mm in radius, nearly parallel ($z_R=5$ m), meets a lens of $f=100$ mm. It focuses to a waist of 20.1 µm, $f$ beyond the lens, as the estimate for a parallel beam of radius $w$ says: $$w_0'\approx\frac{\lambda f}{\pi w}.$$ The light there is 2500 times as intense as in the beam before the lens (the picture overexposes it: lower the brightness to see its shape). Type $w_0=2000$ µm: the spot halves, to 10.1 µm. A wider beam converges at a steeper angle, and the angle sets the spot, $w_0'\theta'=\lambda/\pi$. The focus is short, too: its length $2z_R'=4.0$ mm is the depth of focus. (Spots near a micrometre need more than paraxial optics: $\theta$ is no longer small.)

4. The Gouy phase

Zoomed in on a focus ($\pm4z_R$), showing the crests. The white ticks along the edges are the crests of a plane wave of the same (drawn) wavelength, in step with the beam's crests at the left edge. Follow the crests along the axis: through the focus they pull ahead of the ticks, and by the right edge they are 0.42 of a wavelength ahead. From far before a focus to far after it the beam gains half a wavelength, a phase of $\pi$: the Gouy phase $$\psi(z)=\arctan\frac{z}{z_R},$$ drawn in the strip under the bench (on the axis the field is $e^{i(kz-\psi)}$). It changes fastest at the waist, $d\psi/dz=2/kw^2$: squeezing light into a width $w$ costs it some of its wavenumber along the axis, so near a focus the crests travel faster than $c$. (In Phase the colour is the phase compared with a plane wave: on the axis it turns from red to cyan through the focus.)

5. A telescope beam expander

Two lenses $f_1+f_2$ apart, here 50 and 200 mm (a Keplerian telescope): the beam comes out parallel and $f_2/f_1=4$ times wider, 2 mm, and spreading 4 times less (101 µrad instead of 403: see the table). A wider beam stays narrow for longer, which is why a beam is expanded before it is sent far. The Gouy strip climbs by $\pi$ through the focus between the lenses. Now make a Galilean telescope: click lens 1, make it diverging ($-50$ mm), and put lens 2 at $z=200$ mm, $f_2-|f_1|$ behind it. The same magnification, no focus inside, and no $\pi$.

6. Imaging a waist: the Gaussian lens law

A waist of 100 µm ($z_R=49.6$ mm) sits $s=100$ mm before a lens of $f=50$ mm. Ray optics, $1/s+1/s'=1/f$, would image it 100 mm after the lens, the same size. The beam's new waist is only 75.2 mm after the lens, and 71.0 µm wide. The plot in the panel shows where the lens puts the waist ($s'$) against where it was ($s$), in units of $f$: rays dashed, the beam solid, following Self's law $$\frac{1}{s+z_R^2/(s-f)}+\frac{1}{s'}=\frac1f.$$ Drag the lens. Far from $s=f$ (compared with $z_R$) the two agree. Near $s=f$ the rays go off to infinity, but the waist only reaches $s'=f+f^2/2z_R$, at $s=f+z_R$: you are close to that point now. Make $w_0$ small (so $z_R\ll f$) and the two curves merge: a waist that small acts like a point source.

7. A slab of glass

A beam focused by a lens of 150 mm passes through 40 mm of glass ($n=1.5$). Inside, the crests are $n$ times closer together and the beam converges more slowly. The focus moves back by $t(1-1/n)=13.3$ mm, to 162.3 mm (remove the slab: 149.0 mm). In the ABCD law glass of thickness $t$ counts as a distance $t/n$ of air.

8. The stability diagram

Two mirrors, $R_1=1$ m and $R_2=500$ mm, $L=250$ mm apart. Each is described by $g=1-L/R$, here 0.75 and 0.5, and the cavity has a Gaussian mode (and keeps rays near its axis) only if $$0\le g_1g_2\le1,$$ the shaded region between the axes and the hyperbola $g_1g_2=1$. The mode's wavefronts fit the mirrors exactly (the arcs in the picture), and its waist, 221 µm, is 62.5 mm from mirror 1: nearer the flatter mirror. Drag the point: the mirrors change and the mode with them; outside the shaded region there is none. Drag mirror 2 in the picture (or use the $L$ slider): the point moves along the dashed line through $(1,1)$. Landmarks: two flat mirrors (planar, $g=1$), $R=L$ (confocal), $R=L/2$ (concentric), and one flat with $R=L$ (hemispherical), all on the edge of stability.

9. The resonant frequencies, and why TEM₀₀ is not on the comb

Light resonates in a cavity when a round trip brings it back in step with itself. For a plane wave between flat mirrors that means $2L=q\lambda$: a comb of frequencies $q\,c/2L$, the grey lines, $c/2L=600$ MHz apart (the free spectral range) for $L=250$ mm. A Gaussian mode also gains its Gouy phase, $(N+1)\psi_1$ each way for a mode of order $N$ ($N=m+n$ for $\mathrm{HG}_{mn}$), so it resonates at $$\nu=\frac{c}{2L}\left(q+(N+1)\frac{\psi_1}{\pi}\right),\qquad\cos\psi_1=\pm\sqrt{g_1g_2},$$ with the sign of $g_1$ and $g_2$. Here $R_1=R_2=1$ m, so $g=0.75$ and $\psi_1=\arccos0.75=0.230\pi$: the fundamental mode, $\mathrm{TEM}_{00}$, sits 0.230 of the way from one grey line to the next, and each higher order a further 0.230 (138 MHz), wrapping round into the next free spectral range. The $N+1$ modes of order $N$ resonate together. The plot shows two free spectral ranges; $q$ itself is huge, $q_0=789\,889$ at its left edge, for light at $c/\lambda=474$ THz.

Make both mirrors 500 mm: $\psi_1=\pi/3$, and every third order lands on the $\mathrm{TEM}_{00}$ lines ($3\psi_1=\pi$). Drag the point towards the planar corner: the orders crowd onto the comb, as the Gouy phase of a nearly flat cavity is small.

10. Rays in stable and unstable cavities

Three rays bounce between mirrors of $R=400$ mm, 250 mm apart. They stay near the axis for ever. The plot at the bottom right marks where each ray hits mirror 1, round trip after round trip: the hits lie on an ellipse, and go round it by the round-trip Gouy phase $2\psi_1=0.755\pi$ each time. Press Unstable ($R=100$ mm, $g=-1.5$): the rays walk off within 2 round trips. Then try $R_1=R_2=500$ mm ($g_1g_2=1/4$, $2\psi_1=2\pi/3$): every ray comes back to where it started after 3 round trips.

11. The confocal cavity and its degenerate modes

Here $R_1=R_2=L=250$ mm, so $g_1=g_2=0$ and $\psi_1=\pi/2$: every even order lands on the lines of the lowest mode, and every odd order half way between. The spectrum repeats every 300 MHz, half the free spectral range $c/2L=600$ MHz, and any ray comes back after two round trips. Make both mirrors 280 mm: the orders split apart. Then $R_1=260$ and $R_2=240$ mm: unstable. The confocal cavity sits where the stable region pinches to a point, so it stays stable only if both mirrors err the same way. (Changing one mirror alone keeps the other's $g=0$, on the edge.)

12. A cavity unfolded: a row of lenses

Light bouncing between two mirrors of radius $R$ is light going through a row of lenses of focal length $R/2$, one every $L$. Here $R=200$ mm, $L=150$ mm, and the beam sent in is that cavity's own mode, $w_0=139.7$ µm: after every lens it comes back to the same waist, the self-consistency that defines the mode. The Gouy strip climbs evenly, by $\psi_1=\arccos(1-L/R)=0.42\pi$ per lens. Type $w_0=300$ µm: the beam no longer repeats, it breathes, but stays bounded, because the cavity is stable. Drag one lens along the bench and watch the disturbance travel on down the row.

13. Mode matching

A cavity ($R_1=R_2=300$ mm, $L=200$ mm, also set in the Cavity view) is placed on the bench, its mode dashed in yellow. Only the part of the beam shaped like that mode gets in: $\eta$ is the fraction of the power coupled into it, $$\eta=\frac{4\,\mathrm{Im}\,q_1\,\mathrm{Im}\,q_2}{|q_1-q_2^*|^2},$$ 100% only when the beam's waist has the mode's size and position. Now $\eta$ is 69%. Move the lens: with $f=200$ mm the best is 92% (near $z=362$ mm). Change $f$ too, and you can reach 100% (near $f=254$ mm at $z=324$ mm). This is how a beam is coupled into a cavity or an optical fibre. (The input mirror is taken to be thin, with no lens effect of its own.)

14. Hermite–Gauss modes and their Gouy phase

$\mathrm{HG}_{21}$: two dark lines across the $x$ axis, one across the $y$ axis. Every $\mathrm{HG}_{mn}$ keeps its shape as it travels: the cross-section is drawn in a square that grows with $w(z)$, and only the colours (the phase) change. Its Gouy phase is $(m+n+1)\psi$, here $4\arctan(z/z_R)$: in the side view its crests pull ahead of the plane-wave ticks four times as much as the fundamental's would (click $\mathrm{HG}_{00}$ to compare). This is why modes of different order resonate at different frequencies in a cavity. In the frame that grows with $w(z)$, the paraxial wave equation is the Schrödinger equation of a 2D harmonic oscillator, with $\psi$ for $\omega t$: these modes are its eigenstates, and $(N+1)\psi$ is the phase $E_Nt/\hbar$ of the level $E_N=(N+1)\hbar\omega$.

15. The Laguerre–Gauss donut and its vortex

$\mathrm{LG}_{01}$: a ring of light with a dark centre. Its phase turns once round the axis ($l=1$), so all colours meet at the centre, where the field must vanish: an optical vortex. Away from the waist the curved wavefront adds a phase growing as $r^2$, and the colours wind into a spiral (press Travel). Try $l=3$. Such a beam carries an orbital angular momentum $l\hbar$ per photon. Then choose $\mathrm{HG}_{10}+e^{i\varphi}\mathrm{HG}_{01}$ with $\varphi=\pi/2$: the same donut, made of two modes with lobes (both of order 1, so they keep in step). With $\varphi=0$ it is $\mathrm{HG}_{10}$ turned by 45°; in between, a ring of uneven brightness.

16. Check it by computing

The modes here come from formulas. To check one, Propagate by FFT takes the mode at its waist on a 256 × 256 grid and sends it a distance $z$ with the angular spectrum method of the Diffraction demo, using the exact $k_z=\sqrt{k^2-k_x^2-k_y^2}$ rather than the paraxial approximation, with $w_0=20\lambda$. The computed beam is drawn instead of the formula. For $\mathrm{HG}_{31}$ at $1.5z_R$ it differs from the formula by 0.1% of its peak, and its Gouy phase comes out as $5\arctan1.5=1.564\pi$. Move $z$ or change the mode.
