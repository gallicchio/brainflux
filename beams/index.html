Experiments

A laser beam is not a bundle of rays but a wave that stays narrow: a Gaussian beam. Its radius $w(z)$ (where the intensity falls to $1/e^2$ of the peak) is smallest at the waist $w_0$, and grows to $\sqrt2\,w_0$ one Rayleigh range $z_R=\pi w_0^2/\lambda$ away: $$w(z)=w_0\sqrt{1+z^2/z_R^2},\qquad R(z)=z\left(1+z_R^2/z^2\right),$$ with $R$ the radius of curvature of the wavefronts. All of it is held in one complex number, the beam parameter $q=z+iz_R$, or $$\frac1q=\frac1R-i\frac{\lambda}{\pi w^2}.$$ A lens, a mirror, a slab of glass or a stretch of empty space, with ray-transfer matrix elements $A,B,C,D$ (the same as for rays: $B=d$ for a distance $d$, $C=-1/f$ for a lens), changes it by the ABCD law $q'=(Aq+B)/(Cq+D)$.

Three views: the Bench, where you drag lenses, mirrors and glass along the beam; the Cavity, two mirrors that trap light; and the Modes, the beams that keep their shape. Everything is paraxial (small angles: $w_0\gg\lambda$), lenses and mirrors are thin, and a mirror on the bench is unfolded: the reflected beam is drawn carrying on to the right, as if the mirror were a lens of focal length $R/2$.

How the bench is drawn. A beam a millimetre wide and a metre long would be a line, so the bench is drawn many times wider than it is (the factor $E$ is in the corner). Stretching a paraxial beam sideways by $E$ is exactly the same as making its wavelength $E^2$ times longer (the paraxial wave equation keeps its form), so the crests are drawn $\lambda E^2$ apart: only then are their curvature and their Gouy shift true. There are really 1580 crests in every millimetre of red light. The brightness is squeezed, so that a focus doesn't outshine everything else. Drag a lens, mirror or slab along the bench, drag the round handle to move and resize the input waist, drag the background to pan, and scroll or pinch to zoom. Hover over the beam to read $w$, $R$ and $\psi$ anywhere.

1. A beam from its waist

A red helium–neon laser beam ($\lambda=633$ nm) with its waist $w_0=200$ µm at $z=0$. It is narrowest there, and $\sqrt2$ times wider one Rayleigh range away (the dashed lines): $z_R=\pi w_0^2/\lambda=199$ mm. Far away it spreads in a cone of half-angle $\theta=\lambda/\pi w_0=1.01$ mrad: the diffraction of a beam of width $w_0$. Type $w_0=100$ µm: $z_R$ falls four times (49.6 mm) and $\theta$ doubles.

Watch the crests: flat at the waist, most curved at $z=\pm z_R$ (where $R=2z_R$), and flatter again far away, where $R\approx z$, a spherical wave centred on the waist. Turn on Wavefronts for arcs of radius $R(z)$.

2. Focusing: a wider beam makes a smaller spot

A beam 1 mm in radius, nearly parallel, meets a lens of $f=100$ mm. It focuses to a waist of 20.1 µm, about $f$ beyond the lens, and the panel compares this with the estimate for a parallel beam of radius $w$, $$w_0'\approx\frac{\lambda f}{\pi w}.$$ Type $w_0=2000$ µm: the spot halves, to 10.1 µm. A wider beam converges at a steeper angle, and the angle is what sets the spot ($w_0'\theta'=\lambda/\pi$). The focus is short, too: its length $2z_R'=4.0$ mm is the depth of focus. (Spots near a micrometre need the non-paraxial theory: then $\theta$ is no longer small.)

3. The Gouy phase

Zoomed in on a focus ($\pm4z_R$). The white ticks along the edges are the crests of a plane wave of the same wavelength, in step with the beam's crests at the left edge. Follow the crests on the axis: through the focus they pull ahead of the ticks, and by the right edge they are 0.42 of a wavelength ahead. From far before a focus to far after it, the beam gains half a wavelength, a phase of $\pi$: the Gouy phase $$\psi(z)=\arctan\frac{z}{z_R},$$ drawn in the strip below the bench (the field is $e^{i(kz-\psi)}$ on the axis). It changes fastest at the waist, $d\psi/dz=2/kw^2$: squeezing light into a width $w$ costs it some of its wavenumber along the axis, so near a focus the crests travel faster than $c$. (In Phase the colour is the phase relative to a plane wave: on the axis it turns from one colour to the opposite one through the focus.)

4. A telescope beam expander

Two lenses $f_1+f_2$ apart, here 50 and 200 mm (a Keplerian telescope): the beam comes out parallel and $f_2/f_1=4$ times wider, 2 mm, and spreading 4 times less (101 µrad instead of 403: the table below). A wider beam stays narrow longer, which is why a beam is expanded before it is sent far. The Gouy strip climbs by $\pi$ at the focus between the lenses. Now make a Galilean telescope: select lens 1, make it diverging ($-50$ mm), and put lens 2 at $z=200$ mm ($f_2-|f_1|$ behind it). Same magnification, no focus inside, and no $\pi$.

5. Imaging a waist: the Gaussian lens law

A waist of 100 µm ($z_R=49.6$ mm) sits $s=100$ mm before a lens of $f=50$ mm. Ray optics ($1/s+1/s'=1/f$) would image it 100 mm after the lens, the same size. The beam's new waist is only 75.2 mm after the lens, and 71.0 µm wide. The plot in the panel shows where the lens puts the waist ($s'$) against where it was ($s$), in units of $f$: rays dashed, the beam solid, following Self's law $$\frac{1}{s+z_R^2/(s-f)}+\frac{1}{s'}=\frac1f.$$ Drag the lens. Far from $s=f$ (compared with $z_R$) the two agree. Near $s=f$ the rays go off to infinity, but the waist only reaches $s'=f+f^2/2z_R$, at $s=f+z_R$: you are close to that point now. Make $w_0$ small (so $z_R\ll f$) and the curves merge.

6. A slab of glass

A beam focused by a lens of 150 mm passes through 40 mm of glass ($n=1.5$). Inside, the crests are $n$ times closer together and the beam converges more slowly. The focus moves back by $t(1-1/n)=13.3$ mm, to 162.3 mm (remove the slab: 149.0 mm). In the ABCD law glass of thickness $t$ counts as a distance $t/n$ of air.

7. The stability diagram

Two mirrors, $R_1=1$ m and $R_2=500$ mm, $L=250$ mm apart. Each is described by $g=1-L/R$, and the cavity has a Gaussian mode (and keeps rays near its axis) only if $$0\le g_1g_2\le1,$$ the shaded region between the axes and the hyperbola $g_1g_2=1$. The mode's wavefronts fit the mirrors exactly (the arcs in the picture). Drag the point: the mirrors change and the mode with them; outside the shaded region there is none. Drag mirror 2 in the picture (or use the $L$ slider): the point moves along the dashed line through $(1,1)$. Landmarks: two flat mirrors (planar, $g=1$), $R=L$ (confocal), $R=L/2$ (concentric), and one flat with $R=L$ (hemispherical), all on the edge of stability.

8. Rays in stable and unstable cavities

Three rays bounce between mirrors of $R=400$ mm, 250 mm apart. They stay near the axis for ever. The plot at the bottom right marks where each ray hits mirror 1, round trip after round trip: the hits lie on an ellipse, and go round it by the round-trip Gouy phase $2\psi_1=0.755\pi$ each time. Press Unstable ($R=100$ mm, $g=-1.5$): the rays walk off within a few round trips. Then try $R_1=R_2=500$ mm ($g_1g_2=1/4$, $2\psi_1=2\pi/3$): every ray comes back to where it started after 3 round trips.

9. The confocal cavity and its degenerate modes

A mode of order $N$ ($N=m+n$ for Hermite–Gauss, $2p+|l|$ for Laguerre–Gauss) gains a Gouy phase $(N+1)\psi_1$ each way, so it resonates at $$\nu=\frac{c}{2L}\left(q+(N+1)\frac{\psi_1}{\pi}\right),\qquad\cos\psi_1=\pm\sqrt{g_1g_2}.$$ Here $R_1=R_2=L=250$ mm, $\psi_1=\pi/2$: every even order lands on the comb of the lowest mode and every odd order half way between. The spectrum repeats every 300 MHz, half the free spectral range $c/2L=600$ MHz, and any ray comes back after two round trips. Make both mirrors 280 mm: the orders split apart. Then $R_1=260$ and $R_2=240$ mm: unstable. The confocal cavity sits where the stable region pinches to a point, so it stays stable only if both mirrors err the same way. (Changing one mirror alone keeps the other's $g=0$, on the edge.)

10. A cavity unfolded: a row of lenses

Light bouncing between two mirrors of radius $R$ is light going through a row of lenses of focal length $R/2$, one every $L$. Here $R=200$ mm, $L=150$ mm, and the beam sent in is that cavity's own mode, $w_0=139.7$ µm: after every lens it comes back to the same waist, the self-consistency that defines the mode. The Gouy strip climbs evenly, by $\psi_1=\arccos(1-L/R)=0.42\pi$ per lens. Type $w_0=300$ µm: the beam no longer repeats, it breathes, but stays bounded, because the cavity is stable. Drag one lens along the bench and watch the disturbance travel on down the row.

11. Mode matching

The cavity of the Cavity view ($R_1=R_2=300$ mm, $L=200$ mm) is placed on the bench, its mode dashed in yellow. Only the part of the beam shaped like that mode gets in: $\eta$ is the fraction of the power coupled into it, $$\eta=\frac{4\,\mathrm{Im}\,q_1\,\mathrm{Im}\,q_2}{|q_1-q_2^*|^2},$$ 100% only when the beam's waist has the mode's size and position. Now $\eta$ is 69%. Move the lens: with $f=200$ mm the best is 92% (near $z=362$ mm). Change $f$ too, and you can reach 100% (near $f=254$ mm at $z=324$ mm). This is how a beam is coupled into a cavity or an optical fibre. (The input mirror is taken to be thin, with no lens effect of its own.)

12. Hermite–Gauss modes and their Gouy phase

$\mathrm{HG}_{21}$: two dark lines across $x$, one across $y$. Every $\mathrm{HG}_{mn}$ keeps its shape as it travels: the cross-section is drawn in a square that grows with $w(z)$, and only the colours (the phase) change. Its Gouy phase is $(m+n+1)\psi$, here $4\arctan(z/z_R)$: in the side view its crests pull ahead of the plane-wave ticks four times as much as the fundamental's would (click $\mathrm{HG}_{00}$ to compare). This is why modes of different order resonate at different frequencies in a cavity. In the frame that grows with $w(z)$, the paraxial wave equation is the Schrödinger equation of a 2D harmonic oscillator, with $\psi$ for $\omega t$: these modes are its eigenstates, and $(N+1)\psi$ is the phase $E_Nt/\hbar$ of the level $E_N=(N+1)\hbar\omega$.

13. The Laguerre–Gauss donut and its vortex

$\mathrm{LG}_{01}$: a ring of light with a dark centre. Its phase turns once round the axis ($l=1$), so all colours meet at the centre, where the field must vanish: an optical vortex. Away from the waist the curved wavefront adds a phase growing as $r^2$, and the colours wind into a spiral (press Travel). Try $l=3$. Such a beam carries angular momentum $l\hbar$ per photon. Then choose $\mathrm{HG}_{10}+e^{i\varphi}\mathrm{HG}_{01}$ with $\varphi=\pi/2$: the same donut, made of two modes with lobes (both of order 1, so they keep step). With $\varphi=0$ it is $\mathrm{HG}_{10}$ turned by 45°; in between, a ring of uneven brightness.

14. Check it by computing

The modes here come from formulas. To check one, Propagate by FFT takes the mode at its waist on a 256 × 256 grid and sends it a distance $z$ with the angular spectrum method of the Diffraction demo, using the exact $k_z=\sqrt{k^2-k_x^2-k_y^2}$ rather than the paraxial approximation, with $w_0=20\lambda$. The computed beam is drawn instead of the formula. For $\mathrm{HG}_{31}$ at $1.5z_R$ it differs from the formula by 0.1% of its peak, and its Gouy phase comes out as $5\arctan1.5=1.564\pi$. Move $z$ or change the mode.
