Experiments

A classical particle is a point $(x,p)$ in phase space; an uncertain one is a cloud of points, a probability density that flows along the contours of $H(x,p)=p^2/2m+V(x)$. A quantum state has its own phase-space picture, the Wigner function $$W(x,p)=\frac{1}{\pi\hbar}\int\psi^*(x+y)\,\psi(x-y)\,e^{2ipy/\hbar}\,dy,$$ drawn here in colour: red where $W>0$, blue where $W<0$, white (black on a dark page) where it is zero. It behaves like a probability density in one way: integrating it over $p$ gives $|\psi(x)|^2$ and over $x$ gives $|\phi(p)|^2$, the strips along the top and the right. But it can be negative, which no probability can. The black dots are classical particles, drawn at $t=0$ from $W$ (where it is positive) and each moving by Hamilton's equations under the same $H$; their histograms are the black lines in the strips. Units: $\hbar=m=1$.

Drag the centre of a coherent, squeezed or cat state on the plot; the thin grey curves are contours of $H$ at the stationary states' energies, and the dashed one is $\langle E\rangle$.

1. Quantum and classical agree

A coherent state in a harmonic well: a round Gaussian blob, the smallest $W$ allowed, circling at the oscillator's frequency without changing shape. The classical points circle with it, exactly: for a quadratic $H$ the Wigner function obeys the classical Liouville equation, so every $W$ moves like a classical fluid. $W$ stays positive (min $W$ in the readout is 0) and $\sigma_x\sigma_p$ stays at $\hbar/2$. Drag the blob somewhere else.

2. A squeezed state breathes

The ellipse is narrower than a coherent state in $x$ and wider in $p$, with the same area. It turns rigidly, so its shadow on the $x$ axis, $|\psi(x)|^2$, narrows and widens twice per period, and $\sigma_x\sigma_p$ goes from $\hbar/2$ (ellipse along the axes) up and back. Try other angles and squeezes ($r<0$ squeezes $p$).

3. Number states are rings, and negative

The oscillator's first excited state $|1\rangle$. Its Wigner function is $W_1=\frac{1}{\pi\hbar}(2(x^2+p^2)/\hbar-1)e^{-(x^2+p^2)/\hbar}$ (with $m\omega=1$): a ring, and at the centre $W_1(0,0)=-1/\pi\hbar$, as negative as $W$ can ever be. The readout's min $W$ says $-1.00/\pi\hbar$. Raise $n$: $|n\rangle$ has $n+1$ rings of alternating sign, with $W_n(0,0)=(-1)^n/\pi\hbar$, and its outer ring lies on the contour $H=E_n$. Nothing moves: a stationary state's $W$ is stationary. The classical points (from the Husimi $Q$ here, since $W$ is not a probability) circle round the ring for ever.

4. A cat state's fringes

Two coherent states, at $\pm x_0$, added: $|\alpha\rangle+e^{i\varphi}|{-\alpha}\rangle$. Besides the two blobs, $W$ has fringes between them, positive and negative, at spacing $\pi\hbar/x_0$ in $p$. They encode the coherence between the blobs: a mixture (half a chance of each, no superposition) would have the blobs and no fringes. Change the phase $\varphi$: the fringes slide; at $\varphi=180°$ the centre $W(0,0)=-1/\pi\hbar$ (an odd state). The whole picture rotates rigidly, fringes and all.

5. The marginals are what you measure

The same cat a quarter period on: the two blobs now have momenta $\pm p_0$ and sit on top of each other in $x$. $|\psi(x)|^2$ along the top has interference fringes: two waves with opposite momenta make a standing wave. Those fringes are the sum of $W$ over $p$ along each vertical line, positive and negative stripes together. The momentum distribution at the right has none. Press Play and watch the fringes pass from one marginal to the other as the picture turns. No measurement ever sees $W$ itself, only its shadows.

6. Free motion shears phase space

With no force, every point moves at its own speed: $x\to x+pt/m$, $p$ fixed. The blob is sheared into a tilted ellipse, ever longer and thinner. Its shadow on $x$ widens (the packet spreads), its shadow on $p$ never changes, and its area never changes either. Again the classical points follow $W$ exactly.

7. Where quantum and classical part

The same coherent state in an anharmonic well, $V=\tfrac12x^2+\lambda x^4$, where bigger orbits take less time. The outer points run ahead of the inner ones and the classical cloud winds into a spiral, its arms ever thinner. $W$ follows at first, then cannot: it has no structure finer than $\hbar$, and where the arms come within $\hbar$ of each other it makes interference fringes instead, with negative stripes between them (min $W$ and the negative volume in the readout grow). This is the whole difference between classical and quantum dynamics: the Moyal bracket that moves $W$ is the Poisson bracket plus terms in $\hbar^2V'''$, which vanish for quadratic $H$.

8. Stationary states sit on orbits

The Morse potential of a vibrating molecule, $V=D(1-e^{-ax})^2$, and its stationary state $n=4$. $W$ concentrates on the classical orbit at the state's energy (the dashed contour) with fringes inside. The contours are drawn at the energies $E_n$; the area each encloses is close to $2\pi\hbar(n+\tfrac12)$ (Bohr and Sommerfeld's rule), so each ring between two contours holds one state per area $2\pi\hbar=h$.

9. Tunnelling in phase space

A double well, and $(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)/\sqrt2$: the particle in the left well. Classically it would stay there; the classical points do (all but a few with more energy than the barrier, which go over the top). The quantum state goes through the barrier, where no classical orbit goes, and halfway it is a cat state of the two wells, with fringes between them. It comes back after $2\pi\hbar/(E_1-E_0)$.

10. The uncertainty principle as an area

A squeezed state: squeeze it harder with $r$. The ellipse gets as thin as you like in one direction, but only by growing in the other: its area stays $\pi\hbar/2$ (one standard deviation), and $\sigma_x\sigma_p\ge\hbar/2$. No $W$ can be concentrated in a region of phase space smaller than about $\hbar$: a Gaussian bump narrower than that in both $x$ and $p$ is not the Wigner function of any state.

11. Husimi versus Wigner

The Husimi function $Q(x,p)=|\langle x,p|\psi\rangle|^2/2\pi\hbar$, the probability of finding the state in the coherent state at $(x,p)$, is $W$ smoothed by a coherent state's own Gaussian. It is never negative, but it pays for that: the cat's fringes are almost gone, and its marginals (dashed in the strips) are wider than $|\psi|^2$ and $|\phi|^2$. Switch between Wigner W and Husimi Q.

12. Tomography: shadows from every angle

The blue curve is the shadow of $W$ on a line at angle $\theta$: the distribution of the quadrature $X_\theta=x\cos\theta+(p/m\omega)\sin\theta$. In an oscillator $W$ turns, so $|\psi(x)|^2$ at time $t$ is the shadow at $\theta=\omega t$ of the initial $W$: watch the top strip and the shadow agree. A homodyne detector measures $X_\theta$ for a light field, with $\theta$ set by a reference laser's phase; recording the shadows at all angles and inverting them (the inverse Radon transform, as in a CT scan) reconstructs $W$ itself. This is how the Wigner functions of real cat states of light are measured.

13. Your own wave function

Type any $\psi_0(x)$: a square box to start with. Its sharp edges need high momenta, so $W$ is wide in $p$, with fringes and negative regions, and the oscillator turns it all rigidly. Try exp(-x^2/2) (1 + x) or exp(-(x-2)^2) + exp(-(x+2)^2).

How it is computed

$\psi$ evolves by the split-operator method, as in the 1D Schrödinger demo (on 1024 or 2048 points, between walls far outside the window). $W$ is computed from $\psi$ every frame, one FFT per column of the picture: with $y=kh$ the integral is a sum $\frac{h}{\pi\hbar}\sum_k\psi^*(x-kh)\psi(x+kh)e^{-2ipkh/\hbar}$. Because of the 2 in the exponent the momenta reach only $\pm\pi\hbar/2h$, half the usual range, so $\psi$ is first stripped of momenta beyond that, and then the sum is exact. $Q$ is a windowed Fourier transform. The classical points move by the leapfrog method, which conserves phase-space area exactly and energy to a part in $10^4$.
