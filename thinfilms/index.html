Experiments

Light arrives from the left on a stack of flat layers. At every interface part of it is reflected and part goes on; inside each layer the waves bouncing back and forth add up with phases set by the layer's thickness, its index and the wavelength. The plots show the fractions reflected ($R$, blue), transmitted ($T$, orange) and absorbed ($A$, green), for s polarisation ($\vec E$ parallel to the surface: solid lines) and p ($\vec E$ in the plane of incidence: dashed). The top panel draws the layers and the light in them: $|\vec E|^2$ in units of the incoming wave's. The column on the left is the colour of the stack in white light, as the eye sees it, while the selected layer's thickness changes from top to bottom.

Click a layer to select it and drag its right-hand edge to change its thickness, or use the slider. Drag across a plot to change the wavelength, the angle or the thickness. Any material can be typed as a formula $n(\lambda)$, with $\lambda$ in micrometres and $i$ for the imaginary part; every new symbol gets a slider.

1. A soap film

A vertical soap film drains: it is thinnest at the top and grows thicker downwards. Light reflected from its front and back surfaces interferes, and a wavelength is reflected strongly when the round trip $2nd$ is a whole number of wavelengths plus a half (the half from the front reflection, which flips the sign, the back one does not). So each thickness picks out its own colours. Watch the bands move down as the film thins. At the top, thinner than about 25 nm, the film turns black: the two reflections cancel. Real films do this just before they burst. Click the film to see the spectrum at any height.

2. Reflection from glass

Bare BK7 glass in air. At normal incidence it reflects $R=\left(\frac{n_1-n_2}{n_1+n_2}\right)^2$, 4.2% of the light, for either polarisation. Drag the angle up: $R_s$ rises steadily to 1 at grazing incidence, while $R_p$ first falls to zero and then rises. That is why a lake reflects the sky so well when you look across it. In front of the glass the incident and reflected waves make a weak standing wave.

3. Brewster's angle

At $\tan\theta_B=n_2/n_1$ (56.6° for this glass) p-polarised light is not reflected at all: $R_p=0$. The reflected light is then purely s-polarised, which is why polarising sunglasses, whose axis is vertical, cut the glare from water and roads. At this angle the refracted and reflected rays would be at right angles, and the dipoles in the glass, driven along the p-field, do not radiate along their own axis.

4. Total internal reflection

Now the light is inside the glass, going out into air. Beyond the critical angle $\sin\theta_c=n_2/n_1$ (41.2°) everything comes back, $R=1$ for both polarisations. Yet the field does not stop at the surface: it reaches into the air as an evanescent wave that decays as $e^{-\kappa z}$, with $\kappa=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\sqrt{n_1^2\sin^2\theta-n_2^2}$. The readout gives its decay length $1/\kappa$, 147 nm at 50°: drag the angle towards $\theta_c$ and watch it reach farther (224 nm at 45°). It carries no energy away. The readout also gives the phase difference between the reflected s and p waves, which is 45° at 47.7° and at 55.3°: Fresnel cut a glass rhomb so that light inside it is reflected twice at such an angle, adding up to a quarter wave, which turns linear polarisation into circular.

5. Frustrated total internal reflection

Bring a second glass block close: a gap of air between two prisms, at 45°, beyond the critical angle. When the gap is thinner than the evanescent wave's reach, light tunnels across it. The right-hand plot is $T$ against the gap, on a log scale: a straight line, $T\propto e^{-2\kappa d}$. This is exactly the mathematics of a quantum particle tunnelling through a barrier: for s polarisation $E$ obeys $E''+k_0^2\left(n^2-n_1^2\sin^2\theta\right)E=0$, the Schrödinger equation with the air gap as a region where the "energy" is below the "potential", and $T$ is the barrier formula $T=\left[1+\frac{(k^2+\kappa^2)^2}{4k^2\kappa^2}\sinh^2\kappa d\right]^{-1}$. Beam splitter cubes and some fingerprint readers use it.

6. Oil on water

A film of oil ($n=1.47$) spreading on water ($n=1.33$), thinning towards its edge (the picture is a wedge, thickening downwards). The index rises at the top surface and falls at the bottom one, as for the soap film, so the two reflections have opposite signs and the thinnest oil reflects least. But the reflection from the oil–water surface is weak (the two indices are close), so the cancellation is only partial: the thinnest oil reflects 2.1%, just as bare water does, and the colours, which repeat each time the thickness grows by half a wavelength in the oil, are paler than a soap film's. On a wet road the dark asphalt underneath makes them stand out.

7. An antireflection coating

A quarter-wave layer of MgF₂ on glass. The waves reflected from its two surfaces come back half a wavelength apart and nearly cancel. They would cancel exactly if $n_1=\sqrt{n_0n_2}$; with MgF₂ ($n=1.38$) on BK7, $R=\left(\frac{n_0n_2-n_1^2}{n_0n_2+n_1^2}\right)^2$, 1.25% at the design wavelength, 550 nm, instead of 4.2% (the plot is on a log scale). It works only near one wavelength, so blue and red are reflected more than green: the purple reflections of coated camera lenses and spectacles. The picture on the left shows the colour as the coating thickens from nothing to 400 nm; 100 nm is the quarter wave.

8. A Bragg mirror

Alternate quarter-wave layers of TiO₂ and SiO₂: every reflection comes back in step, and with $N$ pairs the peak reflectance is $$R=\left(\frac{n_0n_L^{2N}-n_sn_H^{2N}}{n_0n_L^{2N}+n_sn_H^{2N}}\right)^2$$ for $(HL)^N$ on a substrate of index $n_s$, which approaches 1 quickly. Here, with one more H layer on top, the readout gives 76.8%, 92.3%, 97.6% and 99.8% for 1, 2, 3 and 5 pairs: open Design a coating and slide $N$. The band of high reflection, the stop band, has a width set by the index contrast, $\frac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda_0}\approx\frac{4}{\pi}\arcsin\frac{n_H-n_L}{n_H+n_L}$, here about 460 to 680 nm: choose BK7 glass ($n=1.52$) for H, close to SiO₂, and it shrinks to a sliver whose peak reflects only 15%. Tilt the mirror and the band moves to shorter wavelengths, as the colours of a beetle's shell or a peacock feather change with the angle. Inside the mirror the field dies away layer by layer: the band gap of a one-dimensional crystal.

9. A narrow-band filter

Two Bragg mirrors with a half-wave spacer between them, (HL)^N 2H (LH)^N: a Fabry–Pérot cavity. Inside the stop band, where each mirror alone reflects almost everything, a single sharp line gets through, 1.12 nm wide. At that wavelength the light builds up between the mirrors (see the field: 74 times the incoming $|E|^2$) until what leaks out of the far side equals what comes in. Each extra pair makes the line narrower by about $(n_H/n_L)^2$, 3.3 times: 3.75, 1.12 and 0.34 nm for $N=$ 3, 4 and 5.

10. A Fabry–Pérot etalon

Two thin silver films, 2 µm apart. Transmission peaks wherever the round trip is a whole number of wavelengths, $2d\cos\theta=m\lambda$, so the peaks are spaced by $\Delta\lambda\approx\lambda^2/2d$ (73 nm here near 514 nm; the plot against thickness shows the same comb as the gap grows). They are sharp because the light bounces many times: the finesse, peak spacing over peak width, is about $\pi\sqrt{R}/(1-R)$ for mirrors of reflectance $R$. The readout measures it: 19. Thicken both silver films to 55 nm and it rises to 35, but the peaks drop from 41% to 16%, because silver absorbs a little at every bounce.

11. A silver mirror

Bulk silver reflects over 90% of visible light, nearly evenly (92% at 450 nm, 96% at 600 nm), so it looks white: the swatch is neutral. Its free electrons follow the field and re-radiate it. Near 320 nm, close to the electrons' plasma frequency, they can no longer keep up and the reflectance collapses to 12%: thin silver is transparent in the ultraviolet. Against angle, $R_p$ dips to 91% at a "principal angle" of 71° but never reaches zero: a metal has no true Brewster angle. (Real silver reflects a few per cent more than this fit; see below.)

12. The colour of gold

Gold's electrons absorb blue and green light (an interband transition of its d electrons), so it reflects mostly yellow and red. This is a 40 nm gold film on glass: in reflection it is gold, and the light that gets through is green-blue, the colour of gold leaf held up to a lamp. Switch the picture on the left to transmitted.

13. A surface plasmon

The Kretschmann geometry: a glass prism, a 50 nm gold film, water behind it, 633 nm light. At one angle $R_p$ dips sharply: the evanescent wave from the prism matches the wavevector of a surface plasmon, a charge-density wave on the gold–water interface, and the light's energy goes into it and is absorbed (see the field piled up at the gold's far side). s light cannot excite it. The angle depends on the water's index: the water is selected, so move $n$ from 1.333 to 1.343, as when molecules bind to the gold, and the dip moves from 74.8° to 76.5°. At a fixed angle, the reflected light changes a lot for a tiny change of index: this is how SPR biosensors weigh molecules.

How it is computed

The characteristic-matrix (transfer-matrix) method. In each layer the field is a wave going forward and one going back, $E=a\,e^{ik_zz}+b\,e^{-ik_zz}$ with $k_z=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\sqrt{N^2-n_0^2\sin^2\theta_0}$, and the tangential $\vec E$ and $\vec H$ are continuous at every interface; a layer turns them into (EH)front=(cos⁡δ−iηsin⁡δ−iηsin⁡δcos⁡δ)(EH)back with $\delta=k_zd$ and $\eta=N\cos\theta$ (s) or $N/\cos\theta$ (p). Indices are complex, $N=n+i\kappa$, so metals and evanescent waves need nothing special. Every number is exact for ideal flat layers and plane waves; the materials are the approximations.

Materials: water (Daimon and Masumura 2007), fused silica (Malitson 1965), BK7 (Schott), MgF₂ (Dodge 1984) and rutile TiO₂ (DeVore 1951) from their Sellmeier fits; silver, gold and aluminium from the Lorentz–Drude fits of Rakić et al. (1998), which are good to several per cent in the visible but not everywhere (their gold absorbs a little more than real gold, so its plasmon dip is shallower). Evaporated films differ from bulk: real TiO₂ coatings have $n\approx2.3$, not 2.6.

Colours: the CIE 1931 colour-matching functions (the analytic fit of Wyman, Sloan and Shirley, 2013) weight the reflected spectrum under daylight (D65) or equal-energy light, and the result is converted to your screen's sRGB, adapted so that a perfect mirror is white. The picture is brightened (the Brightness slider) so that faint reflections show their colour; the swatches say how much light is really reflected. The light is unpolarised and coherent across each layer: very thick layers would wash out to white in reality, as sunlight's coherence length is only a micrometre or so.
