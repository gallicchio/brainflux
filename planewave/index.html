Experiments

A plane wave $\psi(\vec r,t)=A\,e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec r-\omega t)}$ has the same value all along each line perpendicular to $\vec k$, and its phase advances by $2\pi$ every wavelength $\lambda=2\pi/|\vec k|$ along $\vec k$. The colours are its phase (red is positive real, cyan negative); drag the arrow's tip to change $\vec k$. This is the BrainFlux PlaneWave applet of 2002, with more to do.

1. Which way does it go?

Choose each of the four forms in turn and watch the crests (the white dot rides one). $e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec r-\omega t)}$ and $e^{-i(\vec k\cdot\vec r-\omega t)}$ both travel along $\vec k$: they are complex conjugates, the same wave with its colours turning the other way (physicists write the first, electrical engineers, with $j=-i$, the second). When $\vec k\cdot\vec r$ and $\omega t$ have the same sign, the wave travels against $\vec k$. What matters is the relative sign: a crest is where the phase is constant, $\vec k\cdot\vec r=\omega t$.

2. At one point, the phase goes round

The little complex plane shows $\psi$ at the centre of the picture: an arrow going round the circle, $\omega/2\pi$ times a second. With $e^{-i\omega t}$ it turns clockwise: that is the sign quantum mechanics fixes, $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ for energy $E>0$. Change the phase of $A$ and every crest shifts sideways: a phase is a position.

3. The real part is what a ripple tank shows

Only $\mathrm{Re}\,\psi=|A|\cos(\vec k\cdot\vec r-\omega t+\varphi)$, in grey: crests light, troughs dark. A real wave needs both $e^{i(\vec k\cdot\vec r-\omega t)}$ and its conjugate; the complex wave carries the direction of travel in its phase alone.

4. Standing waves

Two equal waves travelling in opposite directions, $\vec k_2=-\vec k_1$. The sum $2\cos(\vec k\cdot\vec r)\,e^{-i\omega t}$ does not travel at all: its nodes (black) stay put and between them the whole region changes colour together. This is a string, an organ pipe, a particle in a box.

5. Crossed beams make fringes

Two waves crossing at an angle. Where they are in step the sum is bright, out of step dark: fringes along $\vec k_1+\vec k_2$, spaced by $2\pi/|\vec k_1-\vec k_2|$. Drag either arrow to change the angle: the wider it is, the finer the fringes. This is how a hologram is recorded.

6. Beats

Two waves in the same direction with slightly different $|\vec k|$ (and so, for light, different $\omega$). The sum is a fast wave inside a slow envelope that drifts along: the first step towards a wave packet.

7. Phase velocity and group velocity

A packet of waves near $k_0$, for a free quantum particle, $\omega=\hbar k^2/2m$. The crests (dot) move at the phase velocity $\omega/k$; the packet (triangle) at the group velocity $d\omega/dk$, twice as fast: crests are born at the back and die at the front. On the $\omega(k)$ plot the two velocities are slopes: of the line from the origin, and of the tangent.

8. Deep water waves

$\omega=\sqrt{gk}$: now the group moves at half the phase velocity, and crests run forward through the packet and vanish at its front, as you can see behind a boat or a duck.

9. Light in vacuum

$\omega=c|k|$: every wavelength moves at the same speed, so the packet keeps its shape and crests ride along with it. No dispersion.

10. Faster than light, harmlessly

$\omega=\sqrt{c^2k^2+\mu^2}$, a relativistic particle (or light in a waveguide, or in a plasma). The crests move faster than $c=1$; the packet, which carries the energy and the information, slower; and $v_\text{phase}\,v_\text{group}=c^2$.
