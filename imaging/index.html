Experiments

A microscope is two Fourier transforms. Light of wavelength $\lambda$ passes the object, whose transmission $t(x,y)=a\,e^{i\varphi}$ can dim it (the amplitude $a$) or delay it (the phase $\varphi$). The first lens sorts the light by direction: in its back focal plane, the Fourier plane, light that left the object at angle $\theta$ lands at spatial frequency $f=\sin\theta/\lambda$, so that plane holds the object's Fourier transform $T(f)$. There the objective's aperture, the pupil $P(f)$, lets through only $|f|\le\mathrm{NA}/\lambda$. The second lens transforms back to the image: $$E(x,y)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[P(f)\,T(f)\right],\qquad I=|E|^2.$$ Paint on the pupil, or use the controls, and the image follows.

Colours in the Fourier plane are phases (red real and positive, yellow-green $+i$, cyan negative, violet $-i$), brightness is $|T|$; the parts the pupil blocks are dimmed. The object is drawn as its transmitted intensity $|t|^2$ in grey, or as its phase $\varphi$ in colour. The field of view is $25.6~\mu\mathrm{m}$ across, on 256 × 256 points.

1. Abbe's experiment: no first orders, no image

A grating of period $d=1.28~\mu\mathrm{m}$ sends its light in a few directions only: the orders, points in the Fourier plane at $\sin\theta=m\lambda/d$ (ringed and numbered: white ones get through the pupil, red ones are blocked). With NA 0.35 only the zero order gets through, and the image is blank: an even field, a quarter as bright as clear glass. The light is all there; the grating is not. Slide the NA past $\lambda/d=0.43$: the $\pm1$ orders enter and the grating appears, as a sinusoid of period $d$. Past $3\lambda/d=1.29$ the third orders enter too (equal bars and gaps have no even orders) and the bars sharpen. Abbe (1873): with light along the axis, a period is resolved only if $d\ge\lambda/\mathrm{NA}$.

2. Block the zero order and the period halves

The pupil (NA 0.6) passes the orders $-1$, $0$ and $+1$, and a small stop blocks the zero order. What is left, $E\propto\cos(2\pi x/d)$, has an intensity $\cos^2$ that repeats every $d/2$: the image has twice as many lines as the object (the profile reads 0.64 µm). The image is made by the orders interfering, not by the object being copied. Choose Open to compare.

3. Oblique light doubles the resolution

A finer grating, $d=0.8~\mu\mathrm{m}$: at NA 0.4 its first orders, at $\sin\theta=\pm0.69$, miss the pupil, and light along the axis shows nothing. Here the light is tilted ($\sin\theta=0.35$), and the whole spectrum slides: the zero order and the $+1$ order both get through, and the grating is resolved from two beams. Drag the light about with the Tilt tool and watch the grating come and go. The limit becomes $d=\lambda/2\mathrm{NA}$. A condenser lighting the object from every direction up to the objective's NA (choose Condenser, NA 0.4) resolves it too, with less contrast.

4. A diatom, Abbe's test object

The silica shell of a diatom is pierced by a lattice of pores whose rows are $0.52~\mu\mathrm{m}$ apart; nineteenth-century microscopists tested their objectives on such shells. The dashed rings mark the lattice's six first orders, at $\sin\theta=1.06$. At NA 0.65 they are outside the pupil: the outline and the midrib are sharp, but the pores are gone. Raise the NA past 1.06 (an oil-immersion objective) and they appear. Or keep NA 0.65 and tilt the light (sin θ 0.53, direction −65°): one order joins the zero order in the pupil, and one family of rows appears, as stripes. Then make the pore spacing smaller and find where the pores disappear again.

5. A transparent cell is invisible

These cells only delay the light, by up to 0.3 rad; $|t|=1$ everywhere. With the aperture wide open (NA 1.4) the image is uniform to about 0.1%: $I=|e^{i\varphi}|^2=1$, nothing to see, though the object's colours (its phase) show the cells plainly. Close the aperture to NA 0.25: the light that the cells' steep edges send out at wide angles is lost, and faint rims appear (choose Stretch under the image to see them). The Fourier plane shows why phase objects are hard to see: nearly all their light is in the zero order.

6. Defocus makes it faintly visible

The same cells, with the focus moved $z=1~\mu\mathrm{m}$ past them (the pupil gains the phase $e^{-i\pi\lambda zf^2}$). A cell is thicker in the middle, like a weak lens, so beyond focus its middle is a little brighter (3% at the nucleus) and its rim darker: for small $z$, $I\approx1-\frac{\lambda z}{2\pi}\nabla^2\varphi$. Slide $z$ through zero and the contrast reverses. Biologists did this for a century: a slightly defocused microscope shows transparent cells.

7. Dark field

A central stop, 0.08 NA in radius, removes the zero order (the undeviated light) and the lowest frequencies. Only 0.7% of the light gets through, but these letters, written in phase alone, now glow on black at their edges, where the phase changes fastest. (With Zero order only the whole letters shine, as $I\approx(\varphi-\langle\varphi\rangle)^2$.) Real dark-field microscopes instead light the object at angles beyond the objective's NA, so the direct light misses the pupil: choose Open and set the tilt to 0.6.

8. Zernike's phase contrast

A transparent object $t=e^{i\varphi}\approx1+i\varphi$ is the zero order plus a weak diffracted wave a quarter period out of step with it, so the two don't change each other's brightness. Zernike's dot shifts the zero order alone by a quarter wave ($-90°$, an advance), bringing the two into step: $$I\approx1-2(\varphi-\langle\varphi\rangle).$$ The cells now look dark (Zernike's "positive" contrast); with $+90°$ they look bright. The profile compares the image with this small-phase formula: at NA 0.5 the image is a blurred version of it (at NA 1.4 they agree to within 0.08); raise the phase $\varphi_0$ and watch the formula fail. A dot that also absorbs, with amplitude $a$, gives $I\approx a^2\mp2a(\varphi-\langle\varphi\rangle)$: with $\varphi_0=0.1$, $a=0.5$ doubles the contrast. Zernike, 1935; Nobel prize, 1953.

9. The halo

A real phase dot has a size (here 0.1 NA), and it shifts not only the zero order but the lowest frequencies of the object too, the ones that make up a large cell's broad, smooth inside. So the middle of a big cell fades back towards the background ("shading off") and a bright halo rings it. Make the dot bigger (0.2) and the cells all but vanish, leaving bright rims; make it smaller and the halo fades. Real microscopes need a ring wide enough to catch all the condenser's direct light, which is why phase-contrast pictures of cells glow at the edges.

10. Schlieren: a knife edge

Foucault's knife edge cuts off half the Fourier plane, keeping the zero order. For a weak phase $\varphi=\epsilon\cos kx$ the image is $I\approx1-\epsilon\sin kx=1+\frac1k\,\partial_x\varphi$: the phase's slope across the edge becomes brightness. The cells look lit from one side, like relief (Hoffman's modulation contrast microscope, 1975, refines this for living cells). Turn the knife's angle and the light swings round. With the Condenser on, the knife also cuts the image of the light source, as in the schlieren cameras that photograph shock waves and hot air.

11. The hidden portrait

Fourier's portrait written in phase alone, 0.3 rad at the darkest ink: the image shows almost nothing (a contrast of 1.5%). Choose Phase dot in the Fourier plane and he appears. The $-90°$ dot shows him as engraved, $+90°$ as a negative.

12. Coherent or incoherent light

The bar target in coherent light at NA 0.5: bars finer than $\lambda/\mathrm{NA}=1.1~\mu\mathrm{m}$ are gone (the 1 µm group is a grey block), and edges ring. Beside a single straight edge the light overshoots by 19% (try Edge); between close bars the ripples add, and the image reaches $I=1.66$ (see the readout), though nothing in the object transmits more than 1. Choose Incoherent: now intensities add, not fields, and the transfer function is the pupil's autocorrelation (the OTF view), reaching twice as far, to $\lambda/2\mathrm{NA}=0.55~\mu\mathrm{m}$, but falling off gradually. The 0.79 and 0.63 µm groups show, with less contrast, and nothing overshoots: an incoherent image is never brighter than the brightest part of the object. A condenser gives something in between.

13. Where resolution runs out: the Siemens star

The spokes' period shrinks towards the centre, as $2\pi r/18$. Inside the dashed circle, $r=\frac{18\lambda}{2\pi\,\mathrm{NA}}=3.15~\mu\mathrm{m}$, it is finer than $\lambda/\mathrm{NA}$ and the star turns grey. In Incoherent light the circle shrinks by half. Then set the defocus to 4 µm: between $r\approx3$ and 6 µm the spokes come back faintly, with black and white swapped (choose Stretch to see them). The OTF view shows why: defocus makes the transfer function negative in a ring (cyan). This is "spurious resolution", and a reason to focus on something with sharp edges.

14. Two points and Rayleigh's criterion

Two pinholes $0.6~\mu\mathrm{m}$ apart at NA 0.56: Rayleigh's limit $0.61\lambda/\mathrm{NA}$. In incoherent light (two stars, two fluorescent molecules) the profile dips by 26.6% (26.5% for ideal points) between them: just resolved. In coherent light the amplitudes add first: in step, the two make one blob, brightest in the middle; set the phase to 180° and a dark line separates them however close they are. Coherent resolution depends on the object's phase.

Things to know

  • Scalar, thin-object, paraxial optics with perfect lenses: the object is a mask, the light has one polarisation, and the lenses add no aberrations of their own. Defocus is the paraxial phase $e^{-i\pi\lambda zf^2}$; at high NA the true one differs. An NA above 1 means an immersion objective, with the object in oil.
  • The image is drawn upright (a real 4f system turns it over) and at the object's scale.
  • Everything is periodic on the 256 × 256 grid: light leaving one edge comes back at the other. The grating's period is rounded so that a whole number of periods fits.
  • The condenser's light is a sum over up to 40 directions inside its NA (Abbe's own method), added a few at a time, so the image settles over a few frames. Incoherent light uses the OTF and is exact.
  • The image's grey: by default its brightest point is white; I = 1 white fixes clear glass at white, so dimmer images look dimmer; Stretch makes the darkest point black and the brightest white, to see faint contrast. The readout gives the true intensities.
  • The profile's dashed curve is the object, $|t|^2$; for a transparent object it is its phase (right-hand axis) or, with a phase dot, the small-phase formula. Drag on the image to move the line.
