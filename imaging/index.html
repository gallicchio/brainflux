Experiments

A microscope is two Fourier transforms. Light of wavelength $\lambda$ passes the object, whose transmission $t(x,y)=a\,e^{i\varphi}$ can dim it (the amplitude $a$) or delay it (the phase $\varphi$). The first lens sorts the light by direction: in its back focal plane, the Fourier plane, light that left the object at angle $\theta$ lands at spatial frequency $f=\sin\theta/\lambda$, so that plane holds the object's Fourier transform $T(f)$. There the objective's aperture, the pupil $P(f)$, lets through only $|f|\le\mathrm{NA}/\lambda$. The second lens transforms back to the image: $$E(x,y)=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left[P(f)\,T(f)\right],\qquad I=|E|^2.$$ Paint on the pupil, or use the controls, and the image follows.

Colours in the Fourier plane are phases (red real and positive, yellow-green $+i$, cyan negative, violet $-i$), brightness is $|T|$; the parts the pupil blocks are dimmed. The object is drawn as its transmitted intensity $|t|^2$ in grey, or as its phase $\varphi$ in colour. The field of view is $25.6~\mu\mathrm{m}$ across, on 256 × 256 points.

1. Abbe's experiment: no first orders, no image

A grating of period $d=1.28~\mu\mathrm{m}$ sends its light in a few directions only: the orders, dots in the Fourier plane at $\sin\theta=m\lambda/d$. With NA 0.35 only the zero order gets through, and the image is a blank grey: the light is all there, the grating is not. Slide the NA past $\lambda/d=0.43$: the $\pm1$ orders enter and the grating appears, as a sinusoid. Past $3\lambda/d=1.29$ the third orders enter too (the second are missing for equal bars and gaps) and the bars sharpen. Abbe (1873): with light along the axis, a period is resolved only if $d\ge\lambda/\mathrm{NA}$.

2. Block the zero order and the period halves

The pupil passes the orders $-1$, $0$ and $+1$, and a small stop blocks the zero order. What is left, $E\propto\cos(2\pi x/d)$, has an intensity $\cos^2$ that repeats every $d/2$: the image shows twice as many lines as the object has. The image is made by the orders interfering, not by the object being copied. Remove the stop (choose Open) to compare.

3. Oblique light doubles the resolution

A finer grating, $d=0.8~\mu\mathrm{m}$: with NA 0.4 its first orders, at $\pm0.69$, miss the pupil, and on-axis light shows nothing. The light has been tilted (sin θ = 0.35) so the whole spectrum slides: now the zero order and the $+1$ order both get through, and the grating is resolved from two beams. Drag the light with the Tilt tool and watch it come and go. The limit becomes $d=\lambda/2\mathrm{NA}$; a condenser that lights the object from all directions up to the objective's NA reaches it too.

4. A diatom, Abbe's test object

The silica shell of a diatom is pierced by rows of pores $0.52~\mu\mathrm{m}$ apart. Nineteenth-century microscopists tested their objectives on such shells. At NA 0.65 the outline and the midrib are sharp but the pores are gone: their orders, the six dots around the centre of the Fourier plane, are outside the pupil. Raise the NA past 1.06 (an oil-immersion objective) or tilt the light, and the pattern of pores appears. Make the pore spacing smaller and find where it disappears.

5. A transparent cell is invisible

These cells only delay the light, by up to 0.6 rad: $|t|=1$ everywhere. With the aperture wide open (NA 1.4) the image is almost uniform, $I=|e^{i\varphi}|^2=1$: there is nothing to see, though the object's colours (its phase) show the cells plainly. Close the aperture: the light the cells' steep edges send out at wide angles is lost, and faint rims appear. The Fourier plane shows why phase objects are hard: most of their light is in the zero order.

6. Defocus makes it faintly visible

The same cells, the focus moved $z=1~\mu\mathrm{m}$ past them (the pupil gains the phase $e^{-i\pi\lambda z f^2}$). A cell is thicker in the middle, like a weak lens, so beyond focus its middle is a little brighter and its rim darker: for small $z$, $I\approx1-\frac{\lambda z}{2\pi}\nabla^2\varphi$. Slide $z$ through zero and the contrast reverses. Biologists did this for a century: a slightly defocused microscope shows transparent cells.

7. Dark field

A small central stop removes the zero order, the undeviated light. Only light the object deviated reaches the image: transparent cells shine bright on black, brightest at their edges, where the phase changes fastest. Real dark-field microscopes instead light the object at angles beyond the objective's NA, so the direct light misses the pupil: set the filter to Open and the tilt to 0.6.

8. Zernike's phase contrast

A transparent object $t=e^{i\varphi}\approx1+i\varphi$ is the zero order plus a weak diffracted wave a quarter period out of step with it, which is why they don't change the brightness. Zernike's dot shifts the zero order alone by a quarter wave ($-90°$), bringing the two into step: $$I\approx1-2(\varphi-\bar\varphi).$$ The cells now look dark (Zernike's "positive" contrast); with $+90°$ they look bright. The profile compares the image with this small-phase formula: raise the phase $\varphi_0$ and watch the formula fail. A dot that also absorbs (amplitude $a$) gives $I\approx a^2\mp2a(\varphi-\bar\varphi)$: the contrast grows as $1/a$. Zernike, 1935; Nobel prize, 1953.

9. The halo

A real phase dot has a size, and it also shifts the lowest frequencies of the object, the ones that make up a large cell's broad, smooth inside. The middle of a big cell fades back towards the background ("shading off") and a bright halo rings it. Shrink the dot radius and the halo narrows; this is why phase-contrast pictures of cells have glowing outlines.

10. Schlieren: a knife edge

Foucault's knife edge cuts off half the Fourier plane, keeping the zero order. For a weak phase $\varphi=\epsilon\cos kx$ the image is $I\approx1-\epsilon\sin kx=1+\frac1k\,\partial_x\varphi$: the phase's slope across the edge becomes brightness. These letters, a pure phase, look lit from one side, like relief. Turn the knife's angle and the light swings round. With the Condenser on, the knife also cuts the image of the light source, as in the schlieren cameras that photograph shock waves and hot air.

11. The hidden portrait

Fourier's portrait, written in phase alone (0.3 rad at the darkest ink): in the image, nothing. The phase dot is off; choose Phase dot in the Fourier plane and he appears. The $-90°$ dot shows him as engraved, $+90°$ as a negative.

12. Coherent or incoherent light

The bar target in coherent light at NA 0.5: bars finer than $\lambda/\mathrm{NA}=1.1~\mu\mathrm{m}$ are gone, and edges ring (in the profile, the light overshoots by up to 19% beside each edge). Switch to Incoherent: now intensities, not fields, add; the transfer function is the pupil's autocorrelation (choose the OTF view), reaching twice as far, to $\lambda/2\mathrm{NA}$, but falling off gradually. Finer bars show, with less contrast, and nothing overshoots: an incoherent image never exceeds the object's brightest part. A condenser in between gives partial coherence.

13. Where resolution runs out: the Siemens star

The spokes' period shrinks towards the centre, $2\pi r/18$. Inside the dashed circle it is finer than the cutoff, and the star turns grey. Then defocus by $1~\mu\mathrm{m}$ in incoherent light: rings appear in which the spokes are back but black and white are swapped. The OTF view shows why: defocus makes the transfer function negative in rings (cyan), "spurious resolution".

14. Two points and Rayleigh's criterion

Two pinholes $0.6~\mu\mathrm{m}$ apart at NA 0.56, which is Rayleigh's limit $0.61\lambda/\mathrm{NA}$. In incoherent light (two stars, two fluorescent molecules) the profile dips by 26% between them: just resolved. In coherent light the amplitudes add first: in step, the two make one blob, brightest in the middle; set the phase to 180° and a dark line separates them however close they are. Coherent resolution depends on the object's phase.

Things to know

  • Scalar, thin-object, paraxial optics with perfect lenses: the object is a mask, light is one polarisation, and the lenses add no aberration of their own. Defocus is the paraxial phase $e^{-i\pi\lambda zf^2}$; at high NA the true one differs.
  • The image is shown upright (a real 4f system turns it over) and at the object's scale.
  • Everything is periodic on the 256 × 256 grid: light leaving one edge comes back on the other. The grating's period is rounded so that a whole number of periods fits.
  • The condenser's light is a sum over up to 40 directions inside its NA (Abbe's method), added a few at a time; the image settles within a second. Incoherent light uses the OTF, so it is exact.
  • The image's grey is scaled so that its brightest point is white (turn off Auto to fix 1, the clear-glass intensity, at white instead).
