Experiments

Two pictures of the same thing. On the left an image $f(x,y)$; on the right its two-dimensional discrete Fourier transform $$F(k_x,k_y)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x,y} f(x,y)\,e^{-2\pi i\,(k_x x+k_y y)/N}$$ which says how much of each plane wave $e^{2\pi i (k_x x + k_y y)/N}$ the image contains. Paint on either side and the other is recomputed as you paint. The origin of both is the centre pixel; $k$ counts the cycles a wave makes across the whole image.

Both sides are complex. Brightness is the magnitude, from black (zero) to white (very large), and colour is the phase:

1. Take the stripes off Fourier's jacket

The engraver shaded the jacket with parallel lines. To a Fourier transform, a family of lines with one spacing and one direction is a pair of bright spots at $\pm\vec k$, away from the centre, at a distance set by the spacing and in the direction across the lines. Erase those spots (with Mirror on, each stroke erases its partner at $-\vec k$ too) and the stripes fade from the image while the rest stays sharp. This is a notch filter.

2. Low-pass filter: blur

With the mask tool, press on the centre of the transform and drag outwards: everything farther from the centre than the pointer is set to zero, as you drag. Only long wavelengths survive, and the image blurs; drag back out and it sharpens. The faint rings near sharp edges are ringing, the price of cutting the spectrum off abruptly.

3. High-pass filter: edges

Now the mask zeroes the inside instead. Without the low frequencies, smooth areas lose their brightness and only edges, curls of hair and hatching remain. Smaller masks remove less.

4. A Gaussian transforms into a Gaussian

Move the Size slider. A Gaussian of width $\sigma$ transforms into a Gaussian of width $N/(2\pi\sigma)$: the narrower one is, the wider the other. This is the uncertainty principle of quantum mechanics, $\sigma_x\sigma_p\ge\hbar/2$, in disguise, and the Gaussian is the shape that makes the product smallest. Paint a single pixel: its transform is flat.

5. Shifting multiplies by a phase

Drag the image with the hand tool. The magnitude of the transform does not change at all (it is being drawn as magnitude only); only its phase does, by $e^{-2\pi i\,\vec k\cdot\vec a/N}$ for a shift $\vec a$. Switch the transform's drawing back to magnitude and phase to see the colour ramp: stripes perpendicular to the shift, closer together the farther you drag. Drag the transform instead, and the image gets the phase ramp.

6. A square becomes a sinc

The transform of a square is $\mathrm{sinc}(k_x)\,\mathrm{sinc}(k_y)$: a bright centre with lobes along the axes falling off as $1/k$. Raise the transform's brightness to see the faint lobes off the axes; lower it to see how much brighter the centre is. Shrink the square and the sinc spreads. Try the disk (the Airy pattern) and the ring too.

7. Slits, gratings and diffraction

Far from an aperture, the light it lets through makes the pattern $|F|^2$: the Fourier transform is Fraunhofer diffraction. The transform is drawn as intensity $|F|^2$. A single slit gives a wide envelope across the slit; two slits put fringes inside it, spaced by the inverse of the separation; seven slits sharpen the fringes into the orders of a grating.

8. Crystals and the reciprocal lattice

A lattice of atoms with spacing $a$ transforms into a lattice of spots with spacing $N/a$: the reciprocal lattice of X-ray and electron diffraction. Make the lattice finer and the spots move apart. The hexagonal lattice's reciprocal lattice is hexagonal and rotated by 30°. In the brick wall, some spots are missing: the bricks' offset rows cancel them (a structure factor).

9. Real images have symmetric transforms

A real image has $F(-\vec k)=F(\vec k)^*$: points opposite each other through the centre have the same brightness and opposite phases. Red faces red and cyan faces cyan (a real number is its own conjugate), but yellow-green ($+i$) faces violet ($-i$), and orange faces magenta. Fourier's portrait has every colour because it is not symmetric. An image that a half turn about the centre leaves unchanged, $f(-\vec r)=f(\vec r)$, has a real transform: load the square or the disk and you see only red, with cyan where the transform is negative (the sinc's side lobes, the Airy rings). In general, the symmetric part of an image makes the real part of its transform, and the antisymmetric part the imaginary part.

Paint on the transform with Mirror off and the image picks up colour: it has become complex. Turn Mirror on, and every stroke is copied to $-\vec k$ with the conjugate value: the image stays real.

10. Phase or magnitude: which one carries the picture?

The transform's phase has been thrown away (Keep only the magnitude): the image is unrecognisable. Undo, and choose Keep only the phase from the transform's menu instead: Fourier is still there. The phases say where the edges are. X-ray crystallography can measure only $|F|^2$, which is why finding the phases (the phase problem) is the hard part of solving a crystal structure.

11. Swap the phases of two pictures

The transform of the author's photograph has been given the phases of Fourier's transform, keeping its own magnitudes. The result looks like Fourier, not like the author. (The menus do this: Remember this on one transform, load another picture, then Use remembered phase. Try it the other way round.)

12. Every point of k-space is a plane wave

One bright pixel in the transform is one plane wave $e^{2\pi i\,\vec k\cdot\vec r/N}$ in the image: stripes of rotating colour, perpendicular to $\vec k$, with $|\vec k|$ of them across the image. The transform is shown magnified 8 times. Drag the pixel with the hand tool and the stripes turn and change spacing. Paint a second pixel (with the brush, size 1) and two waves interfere.

13. Sampling: painting on a lattice

With Repeat 4×4, every brush mark is copied onto a 4×4 lattice: the image is the mark convolved with a comb. Its transform is the mark's transform multiplied by a comb: zero except at every fourth frequency. Periodic in one domain, discrete (sampled) in the other.

Things to know

  • Both pictures wrap around: the discrete transform treats the image as one tile of a periodic wallpaper, so paint that leaves one edge comes back on the other.
  • The transform here is unitary: $\sum|f|^2=\sum|F|^2$ (Parseval), so equal colours mean equal values on both sides.
  • Keys: H hand, B brush, E eraser, M mask, [ ] brush size, Ctrl+Z undo.
  • Your own pictures never leave your computer; everything is computed in the page.
