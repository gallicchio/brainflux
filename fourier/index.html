Harmonics

Experiments

Any periodic wave of period $T$ is a sum of sines and cosines at whole multiples of the fundamental frequency $f_1=1/T$: $$f(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}A_n\cos(2\pi nf_1t-\varphi_n).$$ The top plot is one period; the bars below are the harmonics, their height the amplitude $A_n$ and their colour the phase $\varphi_n$ (the colour wheel of the other demos). Draw on the wave and the bars follow; drag the bars and the wave follows. Press Play sound to hear it at $f_1$ (use headphones, at a low volume).

1. Building a square wave

A square wave has only odd harmonics, with amplitudes $4/\pi n$. Lower the number of terms to 1: a pure tone. Raise it one term at a time: the corners sharpen and the sound gets brighter and buzzier. The bump next to each jump never goes away: it shrinks in width but stays 9% high (the Gibbs phenomenon).

2. The ear doesn't hear phase

Press Scramble phases, several times. The wave changes beyond recognition, but the sound hardly changes at all: the ear hears the amplitudes of the harmonics, not how they line up (Ohm's acoustic law). Contrast the 2D Fourier demo, where the phases carry the picture.

3. Brightness is the high harmonics

A sawtooth has every harmonic, falling as $1/n$; a triangle only the odd ones, falling as $1/n^2$. The sawtooth sounds bright, the triangle mellow, close to a pure tone. Press Remove the top half (a low-pass filter) and listen to the sound grow duller.

4. Draw your own

Draw a period of any shape you like on the top plot and listen to it. Smooth shapes have few harmonics and sound pure; jumps and corners add high harmonics and sound harsh. (A jump makes the amplitudes fall as $1/n$, a corner as $1/n^2$, a smooth curve faster.)

5. A train of pulses

Narrow pulses have harmonics of nearly equal strength up to about $1/\text{width}$: the narrower the pulse, the wider its spectrum (the uncertainty principle again). The envelope of the bars is a sinc.

6. The missing fundamental

Harmonics 2 to 6 at $f_1=150$ Hz, and no harmonic 1 at all. Yet the pitch you hear is still 150 Hz: the ear works out the fundamental from the spacing of the harmonics. (Small loudspeakers rely on this: they can't play the bass notes, but you hear them.)
