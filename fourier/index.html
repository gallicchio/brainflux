Harmonics
Harmonic n is a pure tone at n × f₁, with an amplitude (how loud) and a phase (how it is timed). Harmonic 1 is the fundamental, and its frequency f₁ is the pitch of the note; the bars together are the spectrum.

Experiments

Start here

The top plot is one period of a sound wave, repeated for ever; the bars under it are its harmonics, the pure tones that add up to it. Bar n is a tone at n times the fundamental frequency f₁, its height how loud that tone is (its amplitude) and its colour how it is timed (its phase). Draw on the top plot and the bars follow; drag a bar up or down and the wave follows; press Play sound to hear what is drawn (headphones, at a low volume).

Look for this: the first few bars fix the shape of the wave and the pitch you hear; the higher ones sharpen its corners and make the sound brighter; and the colours (the phases) change the shape of the wave but not the sound.

1. Building a square wave

A square wave has only odd harmonics, with amplitudes $4/\pi n$ (the even ones are zero). Lower the number of terms to 1: a pure tone. Raise it: with every odd harmonic added the corners sharpen and the sound gets brighter and buzzier. The bump beside each jump never goes away: it gets narrower, but it overshoots by about 9% of the jump however many terms you add (9.4% with 5 terms, 9.0% with 15: the Gibbs phenomenon).

2. The ear doesn't hear phase

Press Scramble phases, several times. The wave changes beyond recognition, but the sound hardly changes at all: the bars keep their heights, and the ear hears the amplitudes of the harmonics, not how they line up (Ohm's acoustic law). Align phases makes every harmonic a cosine (all the bars red). Contrast the 2D Fourier demo, where the phases carry the picture.

3. Brightness is the high harmonics

A sawtooth has every harmonic, falling as $1/n$ (amplitudes $2/\pi n$); a triangle only the odd ones, falling as $1/n^2$ (amplitudes $8/\pi^2n^2$: press Triangle to compare). The sawtooth sounds bright, the triangle mellow, close to a pure tone. Press Remove the top half (a low-pass filter) and listen to the sound grow duller.

4. Draw your own

Draw a period of any shape you like on the top plot and listen to it. Smooth shapes have few harmonics and sound pure; jumps and corners add high harmonics and sound harsh. (A jump makes the amplitudes fall as $1/n$, a corner as $1/n^2$, a smooth curve faster.) The period repeats, so if your curve ends at a different height from where it began, that is a jump too. What you drew stays in grey; the blue curve is its first 30 harmonics, with the average left out (a constant is not a sound).

5. A train of pulses

Narrow pulses have harmonics of nearly equal strength up to about $n=T/\text{width}$: here each pulse lasts 0.08 of the period, so the bars first vanish near $n=1/0.08=12.5$. The narrower the pulse, the wider its spectrum (the uncertainty principle again). The envelope of the bars is a sinc, $\sin(\pi n\,d)/\pi n\,d$ for a pulse of width $d\,T$; the cyan bars are its negative lobes.

6. The missing fundamental

Harmonics 2 to 6 of $f_1=150$ Hz (300, 450, 600, 750 and 900 Hz), and no harmonic 1 at all. Yet the pitch you hear is still 150 Hz, their common difference: the ear works out the fundamental from the spacing of the harmonics. (Small loudspeakers rely on this: they can't play the bass notes, but you hear them.)

How it works

Any periodic wave of period $T$ is a sum of sines and cosines at whole multiples of the fundamental frequency $f_1=1/T$: $$f(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}A_n\cos(2\pi nf_1t-\varphi_n).$$ The top plot is one period; the bars below are the harmonics, their height the amplitude $A_n$ and their colour the phase $\varphi_n$ (the colour wheel of the other demos). Draw on the wave and the bars follow; drag the bars and the wave follows. Press Play sound to hear it at $f_1$ (use headphones, at a low volume).

Amplitude and phase, or sine and cosine. Each harmonic can be written either way: $A_n\cos(2\pi nf_1t-\varphi_n)=a_n\cos(2\pi nf_1t)+b_n\sin(2\pi nf_1t)$ with $a_n=A_n\cos\varphi_n$ and $b_n=A_n\sin\varphi_n$, so $A_n=\sqrt{a_n^2+b_n^2}$ and $\varphi_n$ is the angle of $a_n+ib_n$ in the complex plane, which is the bar's colour: a cosine is red, a sine yellow-green, a minus cosine cyan. Dragging a bar changes $A_n$ and keeps $\varphi_n$; Scramble and Align change $\varphi_n$ and keep $A_n$. The constant term $a_0$ (the average of the wave) is left out everywhere: it is not a sound.

Analysis and synthesis. A period you draw is sampled at 512 points and analysed by an FFT; the first 48 harmonics are kept and the rest dropped. The blue curve is the sum of the first $N$ harmonics (the terms slider) and the faint grey curve what you drew. The presets use the closed forms: square $b_n=4/\pi n$ (odd $n$), sawtooth $b_n=(-1)^{n+1}\,2/\pi n$, triangle $b_n=(-1)^{(n-1)/2}\,8/\pi^2n^2$ (odd $n$), pulses $a_n=(2h/\pi n)\sin(\pi nd)$ with height $h=2.5$ and width $d=0.08$.

The sound. The same first $N$ harmonics, with these $a_n$ and $b_n$, are played by the browser's Web Audio oscillator as a periodic wave at $f_1$. Its loudness is set from the power of the wave, $\sum A_n^2/2$ (the mean square), not from its peak, so that changing the phases changes the shape and not the loudness; a compressor catches the odd peak. Browsers allow sound only after a click, which is why the button is needed.
