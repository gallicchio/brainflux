Experiments

Waves from several sources add up. Far away, in the direction $\hat r$, the waves from elements at $\vec r_n$ with complex amplitudes $a_n$ add up to the array factor $$AF(\hat r)=\sum_n a_n\,e^{-ik\hat r\cdot\vec r_n},\qquad k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}:$$ the Fourier transform of the layout, on the wavevectors of length $k$. The first half of this page sends waves out from antennas you place. The second turns the idea round: radio dishes spread over the ground each measure one Fourier component of the sky, and the turning Earth gathers enough of them to make a picture.

Antenna arrays

The elements lie in the plane of the picture; lengths are in wavelengths. The picture is the field at one instant, $\mathrm{Re}\,\psi e^{-i\omega t}$: crests red, troughs cyan. Each element's colour is its phase, and a larger phase means a later signal. Angles $\theta$ are measured from straight up, positive to the right. The polar plot is the far-field power pattern, in decibels down to −40 dB (or in plain power). Below it are the beam's direction, its width between the half-power points, the highest sidelobe, and the directivity $D=4\pi U/P$: the power per solid angle towards the beam, compared with the average over the whole sphere (radiation out of the plane counts too). Drag the elements; double-click to add one.

1. Two elements

Two elements half a wavelength apart, in step. Straight up and down, their waves arrive together and add; along the line they arrive half a period apart and cancel. The pattern is $|AF|^2=4{\cos}^2(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\sin\theta)$: broadside, 60° wide.

Now make the right-hand element lag by half a period, and the beams turn to point along the line

Then put the elements a quarter wavelength apart, with a lag of a quarter period To the right, the lag makes up for the shorter path and the waves add; to the left, path and lag add up to half a period and the waves cancel. Two isotropic elements, and the power goes one way: $4{\cos}^2(\tfrac{\pi}{4}(1-\sin\theta))$.

2. N in a line

Eight elements $\lambda/2$ apart. The beam is 12.8° wide and the first sidelobes are 12.8 dB down. Change $N$: the beam narrows as $1/N$, close to $0.886\,\lambda/Nd$ radians (6.36° for sixteen), and the first sidelobe stays near −13 dB (−13.26 dB for many elements). The directivity is $D=N$: in the beam the waves add to $N^2$ times one element's power, while at this spacing the total power is just $N$ times one element's. The polar plot also has a beam pointing down: a line of elements can't tell one side from the other. That mirror image is not counted as a sidelobe.

3. Steering by phase

Each element lags its left-hand neighbour by $\alpha=kd\sin\theta_0$, so that their waves arrive in step in the direction $\theta_0$. Slide $\theta_0$ and the beam follows, with nothing moving: a phased array, as in radar. Away from broadside the beam widens, roughly as $1/\cos\theta_0$, because the array looks shorter from the side: sixteen elements give 6.36° at 0°, 7.35° at 30° and 13.0° at 60°. 4. Grating lobes

Twelve elements 0.8λ apart. At $\theta_0=0$ there is one beam (and its mirror image). Steer to 30°: a second beam, as strong as the first, appears at $\sin\theta=\sin\theta_0-\lambda/d$, here −48.6°. The red triangles mark these grating lobes, at $\sin\theta=\sin\theta_0+m\lambda/d$; one comes into view once $d>\lambda/(1+|\sin\theta_0|)$, which is 0.667λ at 30°. The array samples its aperture too coarsely, and the pattern aliases, as a signal sampled too slowly does. Arrays that must steer all the way to ±90° keep $d\le\lambda/2$. At a spacing of 2λ there are grating lobes at every $\sin\theta=m/2$

5. Tapering

Sixteen elements in step, all at full strength: the first sidelobes are 13.1 dB down. Now weaken the elements towards the ends (the buttons under Amplitudes). Hann: sidelobes at −31.5 dB, and a beam 9.72° wide instead of 6.36°. Dolph–Chebyshev: every sidelobe exactly at the level you choose (−30 dB), with a 7.98° beam, the narrowest any taper allows for that level; Taylor's taper comes close (−30.0 dB, 8.05°). Binomial: no sidelobes at all, and a 15.7° beam. An aperture with sharp edges has a Fourier transform that rings; tapering smooths the edges, and pays for quieter sidelobes with a wider beam and less directivity (16 uniform, 13.8 Chebyshev).

6. Element pattern times array factor

Four short dipoles, lying along the line. A dipole sends nothing along its own axis: one element's pattern (dotted) is ${\sin}^2$ of the angle to its axis. The whole pattern (solid) is that times the array factor (dashed): pattern multiplication. Turn the dipole axis to 90°, across the line: the dipoles' nulls now point straight up and down, just where the array factor puts its beam, and in this plane the beam is gone. What is left is two lobes at ±50°, and the directivity towards them is only 0.31. The beam hasn't vanished: it points out of the screen, which the plane doesn't show. For dipoles the picture shows the magnetic field, which is perpendicular to the plane; it is exact at every distance, near field included.

7. A random sparse array

Twenty-four elements scattered over 14λ, no two closer than 0.6λ, phased to send their waves straight up. The beam is as narrow as the array is wide: 3.71°. A regular array this sparse would have grating lobes. Here the waves that would have made them arrive with random phases, and spread into a floor of sidelobes averaging about $1/N$ of the beam's power (−13.8 dB for 24; −12.9 dB for this layout), with peaks above it. Press Scatter them again for another layout. Low-frequency radio telescopes such as LOFAR are built like this.

8. Focusing in the near field

The phases $\varphi_n=-k|\vec r_f-\vec r_n|$ delay each element by its distance from the cross, so that every wave arrives there in step: the waves converge on it and spread out again past it. This is time reversal: record what a source at the cross would send to each element, and play it backwards. Drag the cross. The spot is about $\lambda/(\sin\alpha_1+\sin\alpha_2)$ from its middle to its first dark points, where $\alpha_1$ and $\alpha_2$ are the angles out to the array's two ends: never much less than $\lambda/2$. Its brightest point is half a wavelength nearer the array than the cross, because the nearer waves are stronger (the focal shift). Move the focus beyond about $D^2/\lambda$ and it fades into an ordinary beam. Ultrasound scanners focus like this.

Aperture synthesis

Two dishes a baseline $\vec B$ apart, pointed at the same source, multiply their signals and average. The result is the visibility $$V(u,v)=\iint I(l,m)\,e^{-2\pi i(ul+vm)}\,dl\,dm,$$ one Fourier component of the sky's brightness $I(l,m)$ ($l$ and $m$ are small angles east and north of the centre), at $(u,v)$: the baseline as seen from the source, in wavelengths. $N$ dishes make $N(N-1)/2$ baselines, and each gives two points, $(u,v)$ and $(-u,-v)$, because the sky is real. As the Earth turns, each baseline traces an ellipse. The dirty image is the inverse transform of what was measured: the sky convolved with the dirty beam, which is the transform of the sampling alone. The uv plane is drawn over the sky's own transform, in phase colours. Pick a sky, or type one (Typed): $I(x,y)$ with $x$ east and $y$ north in the unit of the field slider; any other letter becomes a slider. North is up and east is to the right in every picture, as on the map; astronomers usually put east on the left, as the sky looks from below.

9. Two dishes make one fringe

Two dishes 632 m apart, a snapshot of a point source. One baseline is two points in the uv plane, so the dirty beam is a single cosine: stripes across the sky, $\lambda/B$ apart (1.14′ at 21 cm), running perpendicular to the baseline. Drag a dish: the stripes turn with it, and get finer as the dishes move apart. That is all one baseline measures: how much the sky looks like one set of stripes, and where the stripes sit (the amplitude and phase of $V$). Six dishes make 15 baselines, and 15 sets of stripes add up to a beam with one peak among many smaller ones

10. The Earth turns

The 27 dishes of a Y like the Very Large Array in New Mexico: 351 baselines. A snapshot samples a six-pointed star in the uv plane, and the dirty beam has six spokes. Press Turn the Earth: over twelve hours every baseline sweeps out part of an ellipse, the uv plane fills in, the beam's spokes fade, and the image of Fourier sharpens. Earth-rotation synthesis was Martin Ryle's idea (Nobel prize, 1974).

An east–west baseline has no north–south part, and its ellipse is $u^2+(v/\sin\delta)^2=B^2$. For a source at the celestial pole the tracks are circles

They flatten as $\sin\delta$, and near the celestial equator they are almost lines: the beam is a streak, and the array can hardly tell north from south

This line's eight dishes sit at the marks of a Golomb ruler, so that all 28 of its spacings differ.

11. Long baselines and short ones

Two point sources 30″ apart, and a Y as compact as the VLA's smallest configuration (longest baseline 1.04 km, $\lambda/B$ = 42″ at 21 cm): one blob. Lengthen the longest baseline: as $\lambda/B$ shrinks towards their separation they come apart, and at 1.4 km ($\lambda/B$ = 31″) the dip between them is down to a third of the fainter one. The longest baseline sets the resolution, as the diameter of a lens does: an interferometer is a huge telescope with most of its mirror missing.

The shortest baseline sets the other limit A disk 6′ across is larger than $\lambda/B_\text{min}$ = 3.1′. Its smooth middle is large-scale structure, which lives at short spacings, and they are not measured: dishes can't be closer than their own diameter, and nothing measures $u=v=0$, the total flux. The dirty image keeps the disk's edges and sits in a negative bowl: it adds up to zero. CLEAN can't put back what was never measured; single dishes and compact arrays fill in the short spacings.

12. An Earth-sized telescope

Nine sites of the Event Horizon Telescope, as in 2018, observing the galaxy M87 (declination +12.4°) at 1.3 mm. Baselines nearly as long as the Earth is wide (12,700 km, $\lambda/B$ = 21 µas) can resolve a ring of light 42 µas across around a black hole's shadow. (The ring is a model, brighter to the south like the one imaged in M87.) With so few stations the uv plane is sparse, a track breaks off when the source sets for one of its stations, and the dirty beam has strong sidelobes; still, the ring shows. Run CLEAN. (The published images used more data and more careful methods.) Try the declination of Sgr A*, −29°: the South Pole station then sees it all day.

13. CLEAN

Högbom's CLEAN (1974) supposes the sky is a set of points. It finds the brightest pixel of the residual (at first, the dirty image), adds a tenth of it (the loop gain) to the model there, and subtracts a tenth of the dirty beam centred there; then again. Two point sources, two hours: press Step a few times and watch the residual, then Run. After 97 steps it has found 0.622 and 0.372 Jy at the two sources' pixels; the true fluxes are 0.625 and 0.375. The CLEAN image is the model smoothed by a clean beam (a Gaussian fitted to the dirty beam's main lobe), plus the residual. Smooth emission (try Fourier) takes thousands of components, and the scales no baseline measured stay missing.

What is simplified

Arrays: the elements are ideal point sources or short dipoles, each radiating as if the others weren't there (no mutual coupling), with no ground. The pattern is drawn in the plane of the elements only.

Synthesis: the sky is a grid of 256 × 256 pixels whose transform is computed with the FFT, so the field repeats beyond its edges (sidelobes wrap round). Each $(u,v)$ sample is moved to the nearest point of the transform's grid, and the third coordinate $w$ is left out (a small field). The dishes' own fields of view, noise, the atmosphere and the source's spectrum are left out too. The Earth is a sphere, and a dish sees the source while it is more than 10° above that dish's horizon.
